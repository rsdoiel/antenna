POSI 2.0 Crossref self-assessment 2026

2026-11-26

In 2020, Crossref’s board voted to adopt the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure, and we completed our first self-assessment. We published our next reviews in March 2022 and December 2024. We are happy to present our latest self-assessment showing there are improvements in a number of areas representing steady progress but there are a few areas to work on and improve.

Overview

Previous Crossref self assessments

  • December 2024 self assessment
  • March 2022 self assessment
  • December 2020 adoption and first self assessment

Background

The Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure (POSI) provides a set of guidelines for operating open infrastructure that the scholarly community can use to assess the openness and sustainability of organizations, initiatives, and services that undertake periodic self-assessments against the principles. POSI is not an organization or a certification process - each POSI adopter (there are now 30) regularly reviews progress, conducts periodic assessments, and self-reports how they’re working towards each of the principles.

Enabling the community to evaluate organizations and initiatives against the principles is valuable but it is also valuable for the governance and staff of  the organizations and initiatives to have a set of principles to identify where they have to adopt new practices, procedures, or policies to make improvements to how they are governed and operated. All the adopters have found the process of doing their self-assessments to be positive.

The POSI adopters have continued to review the principles, reflecting on the impact and lessons learned from their self-assessments. There were some minor revisions to the principles in late 2023 and most importantly, a major update to the principles in October 2025 referred to as POSI 2.0. Crossref has done its latest self-assessment against the revised principles.

Self assessment against principles

Take a look at our 2026 POSI self-assessment at a glance:

Governance

Coverage across the scholarly enterprise – research transcends disciplines, geography, institutions, and stakeholders. Organisations and the infrastructure they run need to reflect this.

Stakeholder governed – a board-governed organisation drawn from the stakeholder community builds confidence that the organisation will make decisions driven by community consensus and a balance of interests.

There are two aspects to this principle to consider. Crossref has a diverse, global membership and with only 16 board seats it’s difficult to have every type of member organization represented. However, over the last few years the board has added members beyond ‘traditional’ publishers. There is currently a funder, two universities, a couple of smaller researcher-led publications, and a number of non-profit societies and associations, based in 11 different countries. Improving representation of members on our Board is the key concern of the Nominating Committee when they set out the representation priorities each year, and then encourage and review nominations in light of these priorities. The slate for the 2026 election had publishers, a society, a funder, a government agency, and a research institution.

The other aspect to consider is that Crossref stakeholders aren’t just the members but include the broader scholarly ecosystem that contributes to, uses, and benefits from Crossref services. Important parts of the Crossref community aren’t eligible to be members such as metadata users, services providers, sponsors, and individuals. However, unless Crossref changes its bylaws about who is eligible to be on the board, these non-member stakeholders won’t be formally represented in governance.

The board periodically reviews the governance structure, and did so this year, considering structural changes to the board. The review didn’t result in any changes at this time, but the board will continue to conduct these reviews periodically.

Our board structure does allow for evolution of the board seats over time. We designate half of the board seats to “large” members and half to “small” members, but how a member is categorized has changed over time. We have 16 membership tiers, and we look at how much annual revenue members in each tier generate. We then categorize members based on which membership tiers generated as nearly as possible 50% of our revenue. This year, members in tiers $600 and above were considered “large” members, and members in tiers $550 and below were considered “small” members. This categorization has changed over time as more revenue is generated from the lower membership fee tiers.

Non-discriminatory participation or membership – we see the best option to be an “opt-in” approach with principles of non-discrimination and inclusivity, where any relevant group may express an interest and should be welcome. Representation in governance must reflect the character of the community or membership.

Transparent governance – to foster trust, the processes and policies for governing the organisation and selecting representatives to governance groups should be transparent (within the constraints of privacy laws).

Crossref has an area on its website with information about its governance including the current board, board election policies and processes, the Certificate of Incorporation, the bylaws, and a listing of motions from all board meetings. Each year there is an open call for expressions of interest from any member to stand for the board and in the election it’s one member, one vote. Starting at the beginning of this year we now post summaries of board meeting discussions and decisions on our blog.

Cannot lobby – infrastructure organisations should not lobby for regulatory change to cement their own positions or narrow self-interest. However, an infrastructure organisation’s role is to support its community, and this can include advocating for policy changes.

Living will – to build trust, organisations should establish and communicate clear commitments regarding their long-term stewardship responsibilities, including the principles by which assets, data, resources, services, and staff would be responsibly transferred to a successor or the organisation or service wound down. The commitments should address future governance, with defined criteria for acceptable successor organisations. This should include continued alignment with POSI and any legal or structural constraints.

Crossref has strong foundations for long-term stewardship, including transparent governance, a board-restricted Capital Reserve Fund, and openly available metadata and code. However, we don’t yet have explicit, published commitments about how our assets, data, services, and staff would be transferred to a successor, or how we would wind down. As a New York State not-for-profit organization and a US tax-exempt entity, there are legal requirements for any transfer of assets or dissolution. For example, members would need to vote to approve major changes and any transfers would need to go to another non-profit with a similar mission.

Crossref is in the process of forming a not-for-profit, international association legal entity in Belgium - an Association internationale sans but lucratif (AISBL). This will be an independent entity with overlapping governance with the US entity and will act as a backstop for it.

What needs to change: the Crossref board will consider adopting a Living Will policy that is consistent with New York State law and POSI 2.0, including criteria for an acceptable successor (not-for-profit status, community governance, aligned mission, and continued alignment with POSI). The board will also consider issues with how assets, metadata, services, code, and staff would be transferred.

Regular review of purpose and community value – organisations and services should regularly review their relevance, effectiveness, and the level of community support to determine whether their continued operation is necessary. If no longer needed, they should take responsible steps to transition or wind down operations in consultation with the community and in alignment with their living will.

This is a new principle that Crossref meets. Our membership and use of our main services are continuing to grow. In particular, content registration, API queries, and Crossref DOI resolutions all continue to see rapid growth thereby demonstrating value.  We do look at all our services and interfaces and deprecate ones that are no longer used or valuable for members. We regularly survey members and the wider community about services and other issues.

Sustainability

Transparent operations - to enable organisational accountability and openness, the operating policies and procedures, detailed financials, sustainability models, fees, strategic and product roadmaps, organisational charts, and other appropriate operational information should be made openly available (within the constraints of privacy laws). Information should be available for investigation and reuse by the community.

This is a new principle that Crossref meets. Crossref provides an operations and sustainability section on its website with financial information, employment and other policies, and membership operations including membership terms, the member application process, fees and billing, and sanctions information. We have a people page with an org chart and a strategy and roadmap page. We also publish our employee manual and organisational policies and procedures. These are available for inspection and reuse.

Time-limited funds are used only for time-limited activities – operations should be supported by sustainable revenue sources, whereas time-limited funds are used only for time-limited activities. Depending on grants to fund ongoing and/or long-term operations fully makes organisations fragile and distracts from maintaining core infrastructure.

Goal to generate surplus – it is not enough to merely survive; organisations and services have to be able to adapt and change. Organisations and services that define long-term sustainability based only on recovering costs risk becoming brittle and stagnant. To weather economic, social and technological volatility, organisations and services need financial resources beyond immediate operating costs.

Crossref has generated surpluses since 2003. Our 2027 budget has a net operating income surplus but has a planned net income deficit because we are spending some of the accumulated surpluses from previous years to invest in our system modernisation.

Establish and maintain financial reserves guided by policy – organisations and services should have a clear policy on maintaining financial reserves, including the purpose, minimum and maximum level, and governance of these funds. The actual level of reserves should be determined and periodically reviewed by the governing body, ensuring that resources are available to support Living Will implementation, including an orderly wind-down, transition to a successor, or response to major unforeseen events. A financial reserve policy might include how funds will be held, under what circumstances they will be used, and how much would be necessary for an adequate wind-down or transfer of assets, given the complexity of the organisation’s infrastructure.

Crossref has a board policy to have 12 months expenses in its Capital Reserve Fund - these are board restricted funds listed separately on the balance sheet. The purpose of the fund is to maintain adequate funds to support a significant change in business or manage a wind down or transfer of operations, should Crossref cease to exist. The Capital Reserve Fund has maintained 12 months of expenses since 2023. Investment returns have grown the fund to keep pace with Crossref’s expense growth. These funds are not being used for the system modernisation project.

Mission-consistent revenue generation – revenue sources should be evaluated against the infrastructure’s mission and not run counter to the aims of the organisation or service.

Revenue generated from services, not data – data related to the running of the scholarly infrastructure should be community property. Appropriate revenue sources might include value-added services, consulting, API Service Level Agreements, or membership fees.

Volunteer labour - organisations that rely on volunteers and their labour should recognise this as a valuable resource for the organisation’s long-term viability, and factor it into sustainability planning and risk management.

This is a new principle that doesn’t apply. Crossref does not rely on volunteers for any of its operations. We have excellent volunteer Ambassadors who support the scholarly community in their locales with ongoing communication, interactive workshops and training from Crossref.

Transition planning - organisations that are heavily dependent on a limited number of individuals should take steps to reduce their dependence on these individuals, including via transition and succession planning, so that the organisation is not at risk of collapse in the event of their departure.

This is a new principle. Back in 2024 Crossref made structural changes to broaden its leadership team to include an Executive team, an extended Director team, and a Senior Management Team, as well as increasing overall staff headcount, to ensure that Crossref could deliver on its ambitious plans and not be overly reliant on any individual or single point of failure in processes. We have clear job descriptions and reporting lines as well as extensive documentation combined with collaborative working so there is knowledge sharing and staff who can cover for each other.

What needs to change: Crossref doesn’t have a formal transition plan for the planned or unplanned departure of members of the leadership team, and the board doesn’t have a policy for an Executive Director transition. We will work on these in 2027.

Insurance

Open source – all software and non-physical assets required to run the infrastructure should be available under an open-source licence. This does not include other software that may be involved with running the organisation.

This was yellow and still is, but we’re making improvements. Anything new that Crossref develops is Open Source in GitLab and there are repositories for older and legacy systems in GitHub. We are reviewing all our GitLab repositories, deprecating some, deleting some, and making any that are closed, open with an MIT license, provided that it is safe to do so. In 2024 we migrated our database off of a closed-source solution and onto PostgreSQL and in 2025 we moved everything to AWS and no longer have a physical data center. This was an important step and lays the groundwork for the rebuilding of the Crossref System. We are investing about $5 million over the next 3 years in a complete redesign of the core Crossref system, which will be fully open source.

What needs to change: the system redesign will ensure that all new code will be open source.

Ensure open and secure data accessibility within legal and ethical constraints– to support potential forking or replication, infrastructure should aim to make all relevant data openly available, following best practices such as applying a CC0 waiver where appropriate. This must be balanced with compliance with privacy, data protection, and security requirements. Organisations should have a clear policy outlining how private or sensitive data will be handled—particularly in the event of a transfer to another organisation—to ensure continuity, legal compliance, and responsible stewardship.

Crossref metadata is made openly available without restrictions. Crossref releases an annual public data file which is available on a 3rd party hosting platform. The Crossref system is hosted in the AWS East Coast US instance and we are also building a staging environment in AWS Germany and a backup in Google Cloud.

Available and preserved – it is not enough that content, data, and software be “open” if there is no practical way to obtain them. These resources should be made easily available with clear public documentation about where they are and how to access them, as well as an open licence where possible. It is not enough that “open” resources are available. In line with the Living Will, it is essential to deposit content, data, and software with at least one trusted third-party digital archive.

Crossref releases an annual public data file which is available on a 3rd party hosting platform and we have archiving arrangements to backup the Crossref metadata with Portico and CLOCKSS.

What needs to change: Crossref’s main system software is hosted in multiple locations for redundancy but there needs to be a more formal escrow arrangement set up - Crossref will investigate this in 2027.

Patent non-assertion – the organisation should commit to a patent non-assertion policy or covenant. The organisation may obtain patents to protect its own operations, but not use them to prevent the community from replicating the infrastructure.

Crossref has a Patent non-assertion policy. Crossref doesn’t hold any patents, hasn’t applied for any, and doesn’t have any plans to file any patent applications.

Prioritise interoperability and open standards to ensure continuity and resilience - infrastructures should adopt and support widely accepted open standards—both formal and de facto—to ensure that systems, data, and services can be replicated, migrated, or integrated with minimal disruption without the use of proprietary extensions or software. Where relevant, organisations should document dependencies on standards.

This is a complicated question and touches on different aspects of the Crossref system and operations. The DOI System is an ISO standard and Crossref metadata is stored and made available in multiple formats including XML and JSON. We have a REST API using standard web technology and standards. Our website is hosted on GitLab and is stored in Markdown and exported to HTML using Hugo - an open source static site generator. Crossref metadata is stored in a PostgresSQL, an open source database. Our system is hosted with Amazon Web Services (AWS) and uses open standards like Docker but we depend on Amazon specific features that would make it hard to move cloud providers.

What needs to change: In the first half of 2027 Crossref will document in more detail its technical infrastructure and dependencies in the Operations & sustainability section of its website.

Conclusion

The Crossref leadership team, staff, and board of directors contributed to this self-assessment and it has been a useful process leading to practical changes and important action items for areas to address. Since our last self-assessment in 2024 we have improved governance transparency by posting summaries of board meetings along with all approved motions and have a greater diversity of candidates for board seats. We have very transparent operations and are maintaining 12 months of cash reserves while investing in major updates to your systems.

Areas where we are working on include developing a formal Living Will policy, improving our transition plans for staff, increasing the level of open source software we use as part of our system redesign project, and publicly documenting our technical infrastructure in more detail.

Please let us have any comments or questions; by emailing or discuss on our community forum.

Good infrastructure is patient work: #Crossref2026 highlights

2026-11-20

Ed Pentz, our Executive Director, opened the 2026 Annual Meeting by introducing the theme: Good infrastructure is patient work. Crossref provides foundational infrastructure: an open, persistent scholarly record of metadata, relationships, and identifiers that thousands of tools and services build on. Maintaining that infrastructure and creating lasting value depends on sustained collective effort. True persistence requires active governance, long-term financial commitment, and organisational stewardship.

Through the two separate sessions of the meeting, we shared updates on governance, improvements to our systems and services, long-term investment to enable a more robust Research Nexus, and our commitment to supporting the integrity of the scholarly record. We also heard from community members about how they’re using and building on Crossref metadata.

Good infrastructure is an inclusive one – and this year, for the first time, we included simultaneous translation of the meeting into selected languages. The recordings are available in English-session 1, English-session 2, Indonesian (first session only), Português-session 1, Português-session 2, Español-session 1, and Español-session 2. All assets, including slides, are available on Zenodo.

In this blog we summarise the highlights across both calls, not in a chronological order of the agenda, but grouping updates in response to some key questions about the patient work behind keeping good infrastructure running.

Table of contents

Who is in the Crossref community today?

Crossref has been around for almost 27 years now and we have built up an amazing community and play a critical role in the scholarly research and communications ecosystem.

Collin Knopp-Schwyn, Membership Specialist, shared an overview of our steadily growing and changing membership. By October 2026, we counted over 26,500 members in 171 countries. 2,774 new members joined us in the 10 months of this year alone. Those new members tend to be small organisations, often based in Asia, mostly registering their metadata with us using OJS. Those are diverse research-producing and publishing organisations, including universities, museums, hospitals, banks, and more.

Sponsors play a pivotal role in this growth: they support organisations that would otherwise struggle to become Crossref members due to financial, technical, or language barriers. We’re also seeing continued growth of members in countries included in the Global Equitable Membership (GEM) program. Collin proceeded to thank our 50 dedicated Ambassadors who help members participate in Crossref more fully.

The growth of members directly relates to the gradual growth of the Research Nexus with more complete and inclusive information about the research enterprise.

Our community encompasses many people and organisations, who are not Crossref members but who use the metadata contributed by our members. Thousands of systems reuse it, resulting in over 2 billion calls to our API and almost 3 billion resolutions of Crossref DOIs per month.

The meeting was enriched with their voices in the Community Highlights presentations. We hosted 11 flash-talks from speakers representing our global community, who shared their experiences and tools related to Crossref metadata. From sharing their journey with Crossref to enhance their metadata practices to building tools that give the community more and easier ways to register records with us, the presenters have demonstrated how good infrastructure depends on shared responsibility and participation.

Who makes the decisions?

You do. Specifically, our Board of Directors is made up by member representatives elected by our members. Every member has one vote, whatever its size. Every year we open elections and invite our members to nominate themselves for available board seats.

We publish our governing documents, board minutes, and (as of this year) summaries of key discussions held at the board meetings to help members engage with the governance process.

Seven of our 16 board seats were up for election this year. 55 applications from 29 countries came forward, and the Nominating Committee reviewed all expressions of interest before building a slate. The Nominating Committee is composed of five people: three of whom are sitting board members, and none come from an organization with a candidate standing that year.

Ballots closed at 12:00 UTC on 22 October, between our two sessions. We had the pleasure of announcing the newly elected board at our second session of the day.

Elected in the smaller-member tier:

  • Bernard Bizimana (HEC Montréal)
  • Cheol-Heui Yun (Korean Council of Science Editors)
  • Marin Dacos (OpenEdition)
  • Pinar Dündar (Scientific and Technological Research Council of Turkey)
  • Mochammad Tanzil Multazam (Universitas Muhammadiyah Sidoarjo)
  • Vincas Grigas (Vilnius University Press)

Elected in the larger-member tier:

  • James Phillpotts (Oxford University Press)

All seven terms begin on 1 January 2027.

We’re establishing a second legal entity in Belgium, an AISBL, alongside our US organisation. Approved by the board, this will give Crossref a presence in two jurisdictions, strengthening our long-term resilience. We’ll share more once it’s formally established. Membership, fees, and your relationship with Crossref remain unchanged.

We also publish how we measure up. We adopted the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure (POSI) in 2020. Thirty organisations have now adopted POSI. Nobody has met every principle, and a self-assessment says how far you’ve got. Ours is due this December.

What do we owe each other?

The growing Research Nexus is a result of a mutual and sustained commitment of our members to each other and to others in the community, which in a basic form is expressed in our membership terms that set the rules of engagement with Crossref through honest and responsible contributions to the emergent commons of the open scholarly record.

We provide infrastructure for our members to register DOIs and rich metadata and to share those openly. We do not assess the quality of our members’ content or how they conduct research. However, as concerns about integrity in research conduct and communications are growing, what the community asks us about now goes beyond metadata: disputed journal titles, paper mills, hijacked journals, authorship for sale. Members increasingly ask what Crossref can do when another member isn’t acting responsibly. So we drafted a set of Member Practices with a working group of research integrity specialists and scholarly sleuths, alongside DOAJ, COPE, ISSN and United2Act, and consulted on them until 9 October. Led by Director of Membership, Amanda Bartell, and Community Engagement Manager, Madhura Amdekar, the consultation asked whether the practices felt reasonable and achievable, and whether anything was missing. Most members supported them; some wanted clarification.

As a result, we updated the wording to offer greater clarity, and we have been slimming the list down since the first draft: from 17 wider practices we went down to nine, which describe practices that we are prepared to revoke membership for. Checklists, how-to documents, and translations will follow. The amended draft goes to the Crossref board in November.

In this place, it’s important to note that even the most responsible members may have cause for concerns about their records when faced with growing systematic deception targeting the scholarly ecosystem. Technical capacity to address the current challenges can be a barrier to maintaining a trustworthy record, and so we’re looking to leverage our experience of partnering with others in the industry on making tools for research integrity accessible to our members (thinking about Similarity Check here). We’re currently running pilots of established tools supporting research integrity with small groups of members, and we’re conducting a survey, in partnership with STM, about research integrity challenges our members face. These will help us determine if there are initiatives in this area that we can arrange in support of our membership.

What does it take to keep the infrastructure working for the community?

Dominika Tkaczyk, Director of Technology, explained how patient work on our technology allows us to lay foundations for future possibilities. Some work of fundamental importance might be invisible on the outside but is essential to keep the infrastructure working. If we move with speed, we may compromise on quality and introduce technical debt. And this can accumulate over time, leaving parts of the system in a fragile state. In 2024, we worked on migrating our database from Oracle to Postgres, improving scalability, reducing operational burden, strengthening disaster recovery, and better aligning with the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure. Our move to the cloud in 2024-2025 also reduced operational burden and made disaster recovery easier – those changes should have been unnoticeable for the users of our system, but were necessary to make it more robust and resilient.

Since then, we’ve built an internal analytical environment – a data lake, which increased our ability to analyse the metadata with ease that surpasses what’s achievable through the use of our API. Instead of embedded filters and facets, the data lake enables running scripts that analyse the entire database. This offers a foundation for versatile reports and dashboards. An early example of a resulting report is our State of the Research Nexus spreadsheet, where we track the progress towards our vision.

Finally, we’re rebuilding and adding functionality to automated metadata matching, to continue offering systematic enrichment to member-submitted metadata, making it richer and more useful to the scholarly community at large. The first example of the new service is the “funder matching” we relaunched in September, which now adds funder’s ROR IDs if they are missing in the newly registered records (where the funder name is available).

“We will invest USD 4.9 million of our surplus funds over three years into rebuilding our system to build a stronger foundation for the future. In expanding our existing infrastructure, we are preparing for metadata matching that would otherwise be out of reach,” said Dominika. This investment was made possible thanks to accumulated surpluses in our revenue that go beyond meeting our POSI commitment to holding a year’s worth of reserve funds (which we achieved in 2023).

Three years is a long commitment, and we don’t yet know how far it will take us. As Helena Cousijn, Director of Programs and Services, put it, ”We expect to get a lot done, and we expect to need patience.”

What did we build and improve this year?

Most of this year’s work was aimed at making it easier to be a member: to join, to register metadata, and retrieve metadata. It sits under our strategic agenda, which has four goals:

Our strategic agenda:

  • Contribute to an environment where the community identifies and co-creates solutions for broad benefit.
  • A sustainable source of complete, open, and global scholarly metadata and relationships.
  • Manage Crossref openly and sustainably, modernising and making transparent all operations so that we are accountable to the communities that govern us.
  • Foster a strong team—because reliable infrastructure needs committed people who contribute to and realise the vision, and thrive doing it.

Helena took a whistle-stop tour of progress made across our programs towards these goals. Developments to improve experience and ability to contribute for our members have dominated this year – from streamlining the prefix allocation process, through improvements in helpers tools and investment in integrations. We’ve seen services transform – from Data citations API endpoint replacing Event Data, to a facilitated transition from Co-access to Multiple resolution. Many new developments have been demonstrated by the teams in more detail.

Lena Stoll, Program Manager for Co-Creation and Community Trends, demonstrated improvements to Metadata Manager, including new record types and the ability to update previously registered records without re-entering metadata. Expanding Metadata Manager’s functionality is central to making the contribution of rich metadata easier for our members. It’s already available for registering grants, journal articles, books and chapters, and post-publication updates, with more record types expected soon.

Lena also provided an early preview of the new Metadata Search experience, expected early in 2027, with improved filtering, the ability to add records to ORCID profiles, citation copying, and improved mobile access.

Paul Davis, Product Manager, shared upcoming changes to Resolution Reports. The new version is expected in the first quarter of 2027, possibly earlier. It arrives as a link rather than a large email, should reach you earlier in the month, and keeps the same content, so anything you already build on it still works.

We also relaunched our Service Providers program this year, providing a framework for collaboration with hosting and publishing platforms that offer a Crossref integration, which our members can use to register their metadata. The program invites such platforms to apply for accreditation at a basic or advanced tier.

What does it cost to keep the infrastructure running?

Lucy Ofiesh, Chief Operating Officer, reported our financial position. Adjacent to rebuilding the Crossref system and as part of resourcing Crossref for future sustainability, we’re also reducing future surpluses by lowering most content registration fees and removing back-year fees from 1 January 2027. These changes follow the recently introduced new annual membership fee tier of USD 200 for organisations with revenue under USD 1,000, and expansion of the Global Equitable Membership program.

These decisions form part of our approach to long-term financial sustainability, balancing investment in our infrastructure with the cost of participating in it.

What does the scholarly record include now?

Metadata developments

Patricia Feeney, Head of Metadata, gave the community a picture of what the record holds today. It’s 187 million records in total. The majority of those are journal articles (almost 68%), followed by books and chapters (just over 15%), and all record types are growing – perhaps the most dynamic changes we’re seeing in grants and preprints. Most of our metadata is optional and we require very little, so what’s in the records reflects what members have chosen to send us.

Patricia talked about metadata developments we’ve seen in the past couple of years, including the addition of the Grant DOI field in schema 5.4 earlier this year, and a number of changes introduced with schema 5.5 last July. The latest schema includes the CRediT taxonomy for contributions, enables greater transparency of medical trials across different record types, introduces blogs and posters as subtypes of the ‘posted content’ category, and more.

We’re now working on schema version 6.0, which will allow members to better reflect the diversity of contributors and the information associated with research. The biggest changes to expect are more inclusive metadata for names – introducing mononyms, more complex names, additional contributor identifiers, and organizational authors. It will also introduce statements covering acknowledgments, funding, data availability, ethics, and AI use, alongside more detailed publisher information.

Metadata enrichment

Metadata continues to change after deposit. As Jason Portenoy, Data Scientist, explained, 80% of works deposited between 2013 and 2021 have been updated at least once. We differentiate five layers of possible enrichments that may result in such changes. At the first two of those layers, members act in their role as stewards and make changes – they may add and update information, maintain links, and help keep DOIs resolving thanks to new information or technical ability to do so, or act in response to community feedback.

One way in which we’re strengthening the scholarly record is by making more meaningful connections between research objects. At #Crossref2026, Program Manager for Contributing to the Research Nexus, Martyn Rittman, demonstrated how metadata matching helps “reduce ambiguities around what is connected and eliminate the guesswork.” We consider this automated process of metadata matching to be a third layer of enrichment.

Funder matching is now making around 15,000 matches each week, and work is underway on grant matching, connecting grant award numbers to the DOIs registered by funders, while our long-standing reference matching process contributed half of the 2 billion citation links present in our metadata corpus.

Finally, we also enrich metadata with an external dataset. This year we made the data coming from RetractionWatch available in our REST API. In case of any changes to metadata records, we insist on clear provenance information that allows the community to scrutinise the changes and decide whether to trust the added information.

These developments contribute to our vision of the Research Nexus.

In summary

Thank you to everyone who joined us, to our community speakers, and to our interpreters, who made it possible for more people to take part in the meeting in a language they felt comfortable with.

This year we focused on the core value we strive to deliver for and with our community: good infrastructure.

Good infrastructure is patient work: interconnected, long-term, and producing something of real and lasting value, made possible through sustained collective effort. You are part of that.

The conversation continues with #Crossref2026 and on our community forum, where the community highlights presentations are posted, and you can reach the speakers directly.

An updated look at Crossref’s carbon footprint - the 2025 report

2026-11-19

In 2022, we committed to balancing our working practices and environmental impact, and to openly reporting on those efforts. This is our third report on Crossref’s annual carbon footprint and reflects as much our emissions as it does our learning process. Travel remains the main area of our environmental impact. In 2025, our total emissions were 170 tCO2e, a 3% increase from 2024, with travel accounting for 85% of the total. A big change this year is that we have greater visibility into our AWS-related emissions. With the full transition of our metadata registry and content registration system to the cloud and significantly increased API use, we’re seeing higher carbon costs to host and run our services.

Here is a quick summary:

  • Our 2025 emissions were 170 tCO2e, up 3% from 2024’s 165 tCO2e.
  • Spending on travel in 2025 was 72% of the 2019 figure, but 56% of the inflation adjusted figure.
  • The Crossref staff grew from 43 in 2019 to 58 in 2025, so on a per capita basis our travel emissions are lower, but increasing staff has meant higher emissions.
  • Hosting our services, now fully on AWS, amounted to 26 tCO2e; that’s 44% higher than the adjusted 2024 figure of 18 tCO2e, which reflects that since July our full metadata registry and content registration system has been on AWS and we vacated our data centre.
  • Our emissions data collection and analysis is still incomplete (e.g. missing figures for database hosting for 6 months, for home-working, and for Zoom, Slack, and other cloud-based services) and it’s all done manually. We want to change this and will look to partner with a 3rd party to help with this for our 2026 report.
  • Overall, we managed to balance in-person, regional, and online meetings in our 25th anniversary year to engage with our global community while still not having returned to the pre-pandemic “normal”.

In 2024, we met the target of keeping our travel expenses below 60% of our 2019 level. In 2025, we exceeded this, with spending on travel and meetings in 2025 at 72% of 2019 levels. The 2026 budget is projected to be 80% of 2019. The value of expenditure on travel as the proxy for our carbon footprint has clearly diminished with the substantial increase in the cost of travel, particularly airfares, since 2019. Using US Bureau of Labor Statistics data from 2019 to 2025, the inflation multiplier for a dollar is 1.3, so the 2019 expenditure adjusted for inflation is $761,000, and 2025 actuals were 56% of this. Based on this, we are still spending less on travel and meetings than in 2019, but it’s more important to look at actual emissions rather than expenditure as a proxy.

Our 2025 emissions were 170 tCO2e compared with 165 tCO2e in 2024, an increase of 3%. From the end of 2019 to the end of 2025 our team grew from 43 to 58 people, so average travel per person was lower in 2025. In 2025, we added 2,800 more members around the world. We strive for all to feel included, and there is a trade-off with travelling. One way to support inclusion is subsidising travel for our ambassadors (who mostly travel within their country or region, avoiding staff long-haul travel), speakers, and collaborators at local events, and having our board members travel to their single in-person meeting (since 2019, we reduced from three to one in-person board meeting per year). This enables participation and closer collaboration, but comes at the cost of an increased carbon footprint.

Travel-related carbon emissions for 2025

Our spending on travel has increased, but on an inflation-adjusted basis we are still below 60% of 2019 expenditure. In terms of emissions from travel, the 2025 figure is 144 tCO2e compared with 147 tCO2e in 2024 - so a slight decrease despite increased staff numbers.

Recorded hotel nights were 437 in 2025, almost the same as 439 in 2024, so we accounted for 7 tCO2e using an average of Europe/US hotel-per-night emissions estimates (Circular Ecology).

The most carbon-intensive activity remains flying, accounting for 85% of emissions in 2025, and about 44% of flight emissions were related to our in-person all-staff meeting held in Split, Croatia.

Remote working

Crossref is fully distributed, with staff in 16 countries who work from home. We continue to use Zoom, Google Workspace services, Matomo, SugarCRM, Intacct, and Slack to support our day-to-day activities and to engage with our community. We were not able to obtain reliable figures to report on the environmental impact of these activities for this year.

We haven’t included estimates of the impact of working from home but hope to do this for the 2026 emissions calculation.

Bringing our staff together for focused meetings is important for the team to function well and make progress in our work for the community, and we’re mindful of the environmental impact. To minimise that impact, we used the ICAO Green Meetings Calculator to optimise meeting locations based on where staff are coming from.

Hosting services

Our database is an essential part of our operations. In 2024, our main metadata registry database was in a data centre in Massachusetts, and we were not able to include the carbon emissions from it in our calculations.

In 2025 and previous years we used AWS for hosting our REST APIs, Metadata Search, and the website. In July, we moved our full metadata database and content registration services to AWS and vacated the data centre. This means our overall emission figures from hosting our services saw a jump this year, reflecting the gap for the previously invisible emissions we weren’t able to report on. In addition, we saw major increases in API use over the year. In 2025, our AWS carbon emissions were 26 tCO2e, an increase of 44% compared with the revised 2024 figure. Based on updated AWS data, we also updated our emissions for 2023 and 2024 to 20 tCO2e and 18 tCO2e, respectively.

AI use

Overall, there are significant environmental costs of using AI tools, which means we expect it to contribute to our carbon footprint too, whenever applied. In 2025, colleagues used AI tools, from spelling and grammar checks to optional use of agentic AI in code writing, but as we didn’t have a systematic way of recording and reporting on that, we’re not able to include the figures here. We’re looking into that in 2026 and, incidentally, will be publishing our staff AI-use policy in the next few weeks.

Tools we used to calculate our footprint

Sources include:

  • Carbon Calculator
  • Circular Ecology
  • Customer Carbon Footprint Tool (CCFT) provided by AWS
  • GreenCalculus business travel air methodology
  • GreenCalculus emission factor checker
  • Claude Opus 5.5 was used to double-check calculations based on data provided manually by staff

Offsetting

Still no. We don’t offset our emissions and don’t have plans to do this. Offsetting emissions is problematic in several ways, so we don’t feel confident doing it.

In conclusion

Considerations for carbon emissions (and the broader environmental and social impacts of our operations) are present across our work. We’re committed to not just monitoring our carbon footprint but also maintaining it at a reasonable level and finding ways to limit and mitigate our impact on the environment.

While the signs of the past couple of years suggest we’ve managed to keep our emissions in check, we’re still learning how to calculate and reduce our footprint, and we expect to continue to do so. We have found our manual data collection process to be error-prone and time-consuming, and it is difficult to figure out which factors to include in calculations. Therefore, for our 2026 report, we plan to work with an external partner.

We welcome comments, suggestions, and challenges to this report and our practices that can help us improve and lower our emissions going forward.
