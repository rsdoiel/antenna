For the first time, the OS will not ship by default with the WoW64 compatibility later that enabled 32-bit apps to run on 64-bit Windows. The last 32-bit version of Windows Server shipped in 2008, with all subsequent releases being 64-bit only. The WoW64 subsystem shipped on 64-bit Windows to allow 32-bit Windows apps to continue running on 64-bit versions of Windows. Perhaps WoW64 becomes something you can optionally remove, but I honestly doubt Microsoft is ready yet to cut 32bit support from Windows 11 by default. I’m not descending into madness because mount and unmount icons make no sense

2026-11-12, updated: 2026-11-21

There are many problems in this world – some big, some small, some so minute nobody ought to put them to paper at all. The problem I’m going to highlight here is even smaller than that. This literally – the actual literally – does not matter and absolutely nobody should care about it, let alone put any work into changing it or addressing it some other way. I want to talk about the iconography GNOME’s Disks utility uses to symbolise “mount” and “unmount”. No, I’m not descending into madness. GNOME’s Disks utility uses the “play” symbol on the mount button, and the “stop” symbol on the unmount button. Inexplicably, considering the state of the world in general and the rapidly deteriorating quality of software specifically, this bothers me. Out in the real world, built on decades of consistent use, these symbols have gained clearly defined meanings, and mounting and unmounting disks definitely isn’t one of them. In fact, the meaning of these symbols isn’t mere convention; they’re defined in a variety of international standards for symbols for use in software and on equipment, such as ISO/IEC 18035 (which you need to buy to read; my copy fell of a truck): ISO/IEC 18035:2002 defines a consistent set of icons and related functions that are represented by multimedia applications on a computer screen and that users interact with to control such applications. It describes controls applying to such functions as: Play, Pause, Stop, Rewind, Scan forward, Scan backward, Replay backward, Loop, Go to beginning, Go to previous, Go to next, Go to end, Set volume and Mute. ↫ ISO/IEC 18035:2003 They’re also part of ISO 7000/IEC 60417, a much larger, more encompassing standard that also contains things like the various symbols used on laundry machines, tumble dryers, in cars, and so on. The symbols themselves are much older than the standards, of course, with many of them dating back to the 1960s and possibly even 1950s. The point remains, though: the play and stop symbols have clearly defined meanings, and I don’t think they are at all a good fit for mount and unmount. The function of the “play” symbol according to ISO/IEC 18035: To enable the user to start the presentation of dynamic media, except where the task requires automatic playing of that media. ↫ ISO/IEC 18035:2003 And of the “stop” symbol: To enable the user to stop the presentation of dynamic media. ↫ ISO/IEC 18035:2003 Neither of these descriptions match what mounting and unmounting mean or do. Mounting a drive or partition doesn’t present any media at all; it merely makes the drive or partition in question accessible. Unmounting also doesn’t eject anything; it merely makes the drive or partition inaccessible. I would argue that play and pause act upon contents of a drive or volume, and not upon the drive or volume itself. As it stands right now, you “play” a drive” to “play” a video stored on said drive. Madness. I already know what you’re thinking, and yes, I agree that the Disks utility is a tool aimed at more advanced use cases and users, and most normal, average users will never even know it exists, let alone open it at all. Very few people get to see these symbols, and those that do already know full well what mounting and unmounting mean, reducing the importance of said symbols considerably. But now it’s time for the big surprise, the twist in the story that turns this entire thing on its head. Files, GNOME’s file manager, uses an entirely different symbol for unmounting! Instead of the “stop” symbol, Files uses the “eject” symbol. You may think this is a more accurate visual representation of what unmounting means, but I strongly disagree. Let’s turn to ISO/IEC 18035:2003 once again: To enable the user to eject the current physical media from the appropriate drive. ↫ ISO/IEC 18035:2003 Unmounting doesn’t eject anything! If you unmount a USB stick, the stick doesn’t get fired out of the USB port. If you unmount a volume, it doesn’t get removed from the SSD. Hell, if you unmount a CD-ROM, it doesn’t even eject it! Ejecting a CD-ROM – opening the drive’s tray or spitting the CD-ROM out of a slot-loader – is an entirely different act! Are we really “ejecting” a CD-ROM twice? Are we “mounting” a CD-ROM when we merely close the tray? No! Madness! The real underlying problem here is, of course, that mounting and unmounting are deeply weird and confusing concepts, bordering on the esoteric, that simply do not have physical equivalents to fall back on. Using symbols and iconography designed in the 1960s explicitly for playing music or stopping a video or ejecting a cassette tape are not a good fit for something as difficult to explain to most people as mounting and unmounting. It’s like putting an eject symbol on a car’s door handle or a play symbol on a soap dispenser. Should GNOME developers waste any time or resources to address this? No, of course not, that would be stupid. Nobody should care about this but me. I should suffer in silence, every time I need to mount or unmount something, forced to look for an eject or stop symbol in whatever user interface I happen to be using. Every time, I should die a little inside. Please, don’t be like me and waste precious resources thinking about this. I’m not descending into madness. The port that does not exist

2026-11-07, updated: 2026-11-15

Ask any program to listen on port 0 and it will hand you a socket on port 47193…or 58037…or whatever the kernel felt like that day. Port 0 is not a port you can have. It is the number you pass when you want the operating system to pick one for you. But a TCP header is just bytes. The destination port field is 16 bits wide, and 0x0000 fits in 16 bits as well as anything else does. Nothing on the wire stops a packet from being addressed to port 0. Your tools will refuse it, your browser will block it, and your scanner’s default port list skips it, because everyone agrees it isn’t a real port. Which makes it a pretty good place to hide things, whether anyone meant to or not. The Ubuntu 26.04 Rust sudo is not compatible with standard sudo

2026-11-05, updated: 2026-11-13

Ubuntu has been at the forefront of replacing the traditional versions of basic tools like sudo with replacements written in Rust, but aside from worries about replacing battle-tested code with brand new, relatively untested code, there’s new things to worry about it. As it turns out, sudo-rs, the Rust replacement for sudo, is intentionally incompatible with the classic version in dangerous ways, as Chris Siebenmann discovered to his dismay. Basically, the classic sudo supports the use of wildcards defined using square brackets, so that if you allow a user to run, say, /usr/sbin/exim -bp, without a password, they can run exim -bpa, exim -bpc, exim -bpr, or exim -bpu without having to supply a password. This allows for very specific, fine-grained access control, which is quite a nice capability to have if you’re managing many systems and users. Sadly, sudo-rs intentionally does not support this, opting to only allow you to use the generic * wildcard. Worse yet, it doesn’t really make this clear, either. ↫ Chris Siebenmann I do not really have words for this. The Rust sudo has apparently opted to keep the single most dangerous wildcard while disallowing all of the wildcards that allow one to be narrow and specific about what you’re allowing, and it buries this at the end of a section that devotes a bunch of words to describing functionality it doesn’t have and doesn’t support, with absolutely nothing else in that section to say this (either up front or in the descriptions of how all the wildcards would work if sudo-rs supported them, which it doesn’t). If you’re going to replace such vital, core tools like sudo that god knows how many people rely on every single day, you better be damn sure you’re feature-complete and can act as a drop-in replacement. If someone switches from sudo to doas, nobody expects the experience to be identical. However, if you’re consistently being told that sudo-rs is a drop-in replacement and you shouldn’t expect any disruptions, you better be able to make true on that promise. “Why don’t we run the taskbar and Task Manager at high priority, so they can remain responsive?”

2026-11-04, updated: 2026-11-12

Another interesting post from Raymond Chen, this time about the difficulty of dealing with a system that’s become CPU-starved. The question is simple: why not just run Task Manager and important parts of the UI like the taskbar at a higher priority? The catch with running anything at high priority is that unless you have been very careful to avoid it, you will eventually run into a shared resource that you will have to wait for, and you are now stuck. Your priority checkout pass gets you to the front of the line, but if the person at the register has a large shopping cart full of groceries, you just have to wait until they are done before you get your chance. Even if the code that protects the shared resource is careful to avoid priority inversion by transferring the priority of the waiter to the thread that owns the resource, you are still stuck waiting, and that owning thread might be doing something that takes a long time, like, say, accessing a file over the network. ↫ Raymond Chen If a system has reached a state where even accessing or using something like a task manager becomes a problem, I personally just bonk my computer over the head with a forced shutdown using the power button. Honestly though, I can’t remember the last time I’ve had to do this, as my Linux machines all seem to handle problematic applications just fine. That being said, I’m sure people managing tons of servers and/or workstations and developers debugging their own running code run into catastrophic problems like this far more often, making forced shutdowns not as practical. Have a terminal window open with top and easy access to the kill command, or make sure you can just ssh into it remotely if the UI has gone completely nuts.
