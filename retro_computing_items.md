## [Jemm v5.87](https://sourceforge.net/p/freedos/news/2026/11/jemm-v587/)

via FreeDOS News · 2026-11-11

Jemm is an "Expanded Memory Manager" (EMM), based on the source of FreeDOS EMM386. Jemm is split into two versions: Jemm386 is the standard version which also needs an external extended memory manager like HIMEM to be loaded, and JemmEx is the extended version that already has an extended memory manager in it. Japheth recently released a new version of Jemm with several fixes, such as + fixed regression in v5.86: exceptions in PL0 were displayed incorrectly + AHCICD: driver now supports long reads in "raw mode" + Int 15h, ah=87h: returns error 02 instead of 80h if limit out of range. See the [Jemm 5.87 release](https://github.com/Baron-von-Riedesel/Jemm/releases/tag/v5.87) page for the full list of changes, and to download the latest version. We've also mirrored this version in the FreeDOS Files Archive at Ibiblio, under [/dos/emm386/jemm](https://www.ibiblio.org/pub/micro/pc-stuff/freedos/files/dos/emm386/jemm/5.87/)

## [JWasm 2.21](https://sourceforge.net/p/freedos/news/2026/11/jwasm-221/)

via FreeDOS News · 2026-11-11

JWasm is a MASM-compatible assembler that runs under DOS and other operating systems. Japheth has released a new version of JWasm with a bunch of fixes and changes, including: + macro arguments: curly braces now handled Masm-compatible + operator MOD returned wrong results for negative operands + model TINY, predefined symbol `@code`: changed from `_TEXT` to `DGROUP` \+ fixed GPF in jwasm: assembling MOV instruction with destination of type prototype, but without pointer type definition. Check out the full change log and download the latest version on the [JWasm 2.21 release](https://github.com/Baron-von-Riedesel/JWasm/releases/tag/v2.21) page. We've also mirrored this version in the FreeDOS Files Archive at Ibiblio, under [/devel/asm/jwasm](https://www.ibiblio.org/pub/micro/pc-stuff/freedos/files/devel/asm/jwasm/2.21/)

## [The classic console corner's CRT changeover](https://oldvcr.blogspot.com/2026/11/the-classic-console-corners-crt.html)

via Old Ventage Computing Research · 2026-11-10, updated 2026-11-20

In the corner of the rear Floodgap lab sits a pile of classic game consoles. It used to look like this:

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiDOsKd7ARFSfeQEdLbBJLWHNxiUDIWNbR_WkJRWpgdB9RWifR0gYW-vDiwyQvj5kgwjrflcA3ZFxIwZSodIftZOrmdM-KhQf1ajhoQgDNuf3MDdx3YWjGe5tYew7o4Dk_n7adfe2q0fHSnvnoSFQ3i-2yYvqKyBlmMqvbqSP9RkNtPgt3VtehDm3eB7gQ/s320/PXL_20261031_172649000.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiDOsKd7ARFSfeQEdLbBJLWHNxiUDIWNbR_WkJRWpgdB9RWifR0gYW-vDiwyQvj5kgwjrflcA3ZFxIwZSodIftZOrmdM-KhQf1ajhoQgDNuf3MDdx3YWjGe5tYew7o4Dk_n7adfe2q0fHSnvnoSFQ3i-2yYvqKyBlmMqvbqSP9RkNtPgt3VtehDm3eB7gQ/s4080/PXL_20261031_172649000.jpg)

The centrepiece is the 20" Panasonic cathode ray tube TV-VCR on the cart (and a DVD player balanced on top), because with the exception of the [Sega Dreamcast](/2022/11/refurb-weekend-sega-dreamcast.html) all the consoles here only came with NTSC RF output and date from the pre-flat panel TV era. That includes multiple Atari consoles (including the inevitable VCS, here a Darth Vader 2600), several Pong machines — including [two Commodore units](/2022/08/the-pong-you-could-program-possibly-mos.html) — with various chipsets, an Intellivision (as the [rebadged Tandyvision One](/2024/12/composite-and-hard-reset-mods-for.html), my first) and a Bally Astrocade with lots of upgrades. It seems wrong to use them with a flat panel, and CRTs lag less. Plus, games with a light gun (here the Dreamcast and one of the Pong consoles) [often rely](https://nicole.express/2023/bang-bang-youre-dead.html) on the CRT's rapidly scanning beam to know where they're pointed.

So what do you do when the CRT TV goes bad?

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjP3Dp81_rVMUi_5yOrUa5Uyfve107EqOLqzKQKcyP6kGwZVAZwzXdhD54t_mTkC9XwOhqXW1dH7-_6jMVcm88AdBpgrfLypW9Ln44om4Au7Mx9sB22blPlnvSU7OtdQyExLU7Pno4ZHcf9CEY2-5ARz51QqQCmeF4wv_7uE-o5TA0gsoq-m_X0PMgCpec/s320/PXL_20261030_220809512.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEjP3Dp81_rVMUi_5yOrUa5Uyfve107EqOLqzKQKcyP6kGwZVAZwzXdhD54t_mTkC9XwOhqXW1dH7-_6jMVcm88AdBpgrfLypW9Ln44om4Au7Mx9sB22blPlnvSU7OtdQyExLU7Pno4ZHcf9CEY2-5ARz51QqQCmeF4wv_7uE-o5TA0gsoq-m_X0PMgCpec/s4080/PXL_20261030_220809512.jpg)

You track down another one, and then labouriously redo all the connections so you can still play the games. Yes, this article is basically just a big bubbling ball of nostalgia. My consoles — let me show you them.

I should note that this isn't all the old school gaming fun in the house. In the commons next to the pinball machines are an Xbox 360 Slim and a PlayStation 2, and in the home theatre connected to the 3D projector is a PlayStation 3 since it has some stereoscopic titles. These might appear in future articles. But most of the rest are here.

I bought this Panasonic Omnivision PV-M2035 from a friend at church in 1999 for $100 \[about $200 in 2026 dollars\]. He had no use for it, and the top of the plastic shell had been heat-warped by sitting in his car too long, but it worked, still had the remote, and was a suitably cheap TV for my unsuitably cheap studio apartment. I used it for local news and _Friends_ with a set of rabbit ears, VHS movies, and speakers for playing music by connecting a CD player to the single rear input. When I moved to a less cheap 1-bedroom, it came along, and its flat top was flat enough to become a shelf for a DVD player. I got the Dreamcast from Fry's in 2001 after Sega discontinued it and managed to share the composite input between them. That apartment was my crashpad for about a decade before moving to the current orbiting HQ, whereupon I bought a passive-3D flat panel TV and moved the Omnivision to its current location in the back of the house. It shared the RF input between the Inty, and later the Atari 2600, and later still the Astrocade and various Pongs. At any one time there were at least four consoles always connected to it via switches and switchboxes, and I wired up an accessory port for more.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgQyGjFVQs9FpTbXaiUlPnPhL3SW6KG0Eqw_5vd_h_8s0HmEyg2N12EiXhIqx8fXWSlsfEnq8bGyfs9Q5dhuJawkpsDSzf64hzw49Xnsqi-vKbgAAyqIeM0XnzglEZv9NBZkcdhI-LNTjZOY6FhdV3bIHDlrj2zDLMgcGlGbI9KdDggeN-5wbTGzhhFXVQ/s320/PXL_20261031_174423324.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgQyGjFVQs9FpTbXaiUlPnPhL3SW6KG0Eqw_5vd_h_8s0HmEyg2N12EiXhIqx8fXWSlsfEnq8bGyfs9Q5dhuJawkpsDSzf64hzw49Xnsqi-vKbgAAyqIeM0XnzglEZv9NBZkcdhI-LNTjZOY6FhdV3bIHDlrj2zDLMgcGlGbI9KdDggeN-5wbTGzhhFXVQ/s4080/PXL_20261031_174423324.jpg)

Last year the screen started doing ... this. It was likewise very jumpy with the Tandyvision through the RF input, lots of roll, and with the Dreamcast on the composite input was very dark and eventually faded to black. Oddly a tape played through the VCR was stable, but it had really bad colour. Although I managed to track down a service manual with the schematics, this TV hails from that awkward transitional era where formerly discrete components were increasingly being consolidated into ASICs, and gingerly probing the board while keeping my fool fingers away from the tube didn't yield an obvious single point of failure like a blown cap. Plus, as a combo TV-VCR, there were lots of paths to the display output and all of those paths were bad in different ways with no clear pattern.

[![](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiqHc9us_iurqsPO7yLIK_wf0G8CBcyMsqN0xX1_mubHx0P8GzuMBeZGjzzhJ3PoLF_-qVZF7k35S3zxor8dNJtDlJLqlhwj7sXHb18AYDQp3vUWKvvYR7jHs1y_0LFxMwJpMYJbzqn2J45GZRECk-TKe_mslAgMXQGdiowKYqH3mUv5ruWbPH0RT3Sm6M/s320/PXL_20261031_181513252.jpg)](https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiqHc9us_iurqsPO7yLIK_wf0G8CBcyMsqN0xX1_mubHx0P8GzuMBeZGjzzhJ3PoLF_-qVZF7k35S3zxor8dNJtDlJLqlhwj7sXHb18AYDQp3vUWKvvYR7jHs1y_0LFxMwJpMYJbzqn2J45GZRECk-TKe_mslAgMXQGdiowKYqH3mUv5ruWbPH0RT3Sm6M/s4080/PXL_20261031_181513252.jpg)

I left the console corner to gather dust (it's good at that) and tried finding another similar Panasonic TV-VCR so I could either swap the main board or simply put it in service. After months of scanning eBay auctions I found this fully working Panasonic PV-M2046 which is nearly the same except for a 4-head VCR (the PV-M2035 has a two-head) and front inputs, and its service manual suggested it had similar or identical components. The seller padded the face of the CRT well, but UPS still managed to find a way to dropkick it off the lift gate or something, because the _plastic_ was broken and on these TVs that's pretty much the load-bearing part. When it arrived at the mailbox and I unpacked it, I could see the lower half had sandwiched right through the VCR slot, with visible PCB fragments and broken buttons; aside from the tube itself (ironically), the thing was a total loss. The seller nevertheless made good on the refund, to their credit, and I got to schlep it to the recycler.

Overall, however, it had occurred to me that there were very few TVs sufficiently contemporary with the M2035 out there, which might be a hint about their general longevity. So, if I was going to be forced to buy a different Panasonic, then it might as well be a more recent one, and it should be one of the "triple play" units (i.e., TV, VCR, DVD) so that I could at least get rid of the external DVD player.

This unit was produced in June 2006, substantially later than the older TV, so if I can't fix that one then I should get plenty of wear from this one.

Because this TV was produced during the lead-up to the end of analogue television broadcasting in the United States, it carries this warning on the front that it "will require a converter box after February 17, 2009" consistent with the Digital Transition and Public Safety Act of 2005. The reason for the warning is because it only has an analogue NTSC TV tuner, predating the FCC March 1, 2017 requirement to have a digital ATSC tuner, though an old NTSC one is exactly what we want for our purposes. The DTV Delay Act of February 4, 2009 later pushed the end of full-power analogue TV broadcasting to June 12, 2009 to buy time for people stuck on waiting lists for digital converter box coupons. I got a couple of these myself and used one for a free tuner with the old Panasonic, though I don't know where the tuner or the other unredeemed coupon card ended up. After that date, only low-power broadcasters and translators remained until the last such stations were shut down in Alaska by January 10, 2022.

Now that we have our triple-play TV, let's look at all that dusty stuff wired into the old TV set before we dismantle it. The Dreamcast and DVD player share the single rear input with a Y-cable each in its video and audio jacks. I'll show you this in a second, but the video Y-cable has one end connected to the Dreamcast and the other end to the box on the left, a Sima Video CopyMaster used to defeat old-school Macrovision Analog Protection System signals when enabled on a DVD. Macrovision inserts abnormal pulses in the vertical blank that interfere with most VCRs' automatic gain circuitry, causing wild changes in brightness and preventing a clean VHS recording. This includes copying VHS tapes encoded the same way, and DVD recorders that detect these pulses will likewise refuse to operate. Since this TV routes the rear inputs through the VCR so they can be recorded, it becomes susceptible even when _not_ recording and thus impossible to watch many DVDs, for which the Sima CopyMaster was the cheapest solution: it simply blots out that portion of the vertical blank along with the pulses, which also has the side effect of sometimes stripping closed captioning (in the vertical blank as well), though DVDs with proper subtitles are of course unaffected. Later Sima devices could regenerate portions of the vertical blank for better quality, and Macrovision actually sued them over their products in 2005, but [later settled](https://www.eff.org/cases/macrovision-v-sima).

The mixer on the right was purchased literal decades ago at Radio Shack (more specifically a "Video Enhancer Stereo Audio Mixer") that I think we used for a Halloween display exactly once and then forgot about. Here it was used first as a cutoff switch, and second as a way to match the audio volume from the DVD player with the audio volume from the Dreamcast, which was also directly connected to the second rear Y-cable. This TV is mono, so the stereo channels from the audio mixer and the DC get merged using their own Y-connectors (also Radio Shack) before going into the Y-cable to the single line audio input. The video function isn't used here at all; it was just a audio mixer on the shelf I wasn't otherwise using. But you haven't met the Bally on the bottom, officially introduced in 1978 as a game console and simple home computer, sold under various names such as the Bally Professional Arcade seen on early units, later the Bally Computer System badging here, and now collectively referred to under its final incarnation as the Astrocade. These machines were based around a 1.789MHz Z80 (half NTSC colourburst) combined with the same graphics hardware Dave Nutting Associates used for Midway's arcade games Gorf and Wizard of Wor, among others. Unfortunately, much of its theoretical graphics power went unrealized because it only has half the video memory of the arcade units (i.e., 4K) — and that's all the memory it has, so it has to interleave bits in the video memory to store BASIC programs, meaning only game ROMs can take full advantage. Still, that was enough to be solid competition for the likes of the Atari VCS and Fairchild Channel F, and it holds its own well against the slightly later Intellivision.

Even amongst contemporaries from that pioneering era, the Astrocade is a delightfully odd duck. Instead of a conventional keyboard, the computer functions are accessed through a small keypad which by default is laid out like a calculator, but with an overlay can also be used to enter letters and BASIC keywords. Because the game cartridge form factor was still a relatively fluid concept, Bally settled on a cassette-like carrier called a "Videocade" that was inserted and ejected (boing!) like compact cassette tapes. The rear of the machine was used to store the Videocade cartridges and covered by a clear smoked plastic shell, but since I have a multicart installed I use the back to store two of the Astrocade's unique controllers, a surprisingly functional mashup of joystick and paddle with a trigger button on the grip.

The Astrocade also has quite a lot of expansion capability. The Astrocade is a solid system and fun to play, so we'll take a spin a bit later, but one big drawback it _does_ have is a doozy: the cooling vents are on the underside. These machines are very prone to overheating and can be easily damaged by it, which is why I use two foam struts to keep it off the carpet.

The yellow label is for Astrovision, a separate enterprise that had been attempting to develop their own console. The (then) Bally Professional Arcade was selling poorly, though part of this was due to a tardy launch and Bally's weak retail presence, and Bally decided to sell off the consumer products division in 1979. Astrovision, unable to get their unit to market quickly, bought it in 1981 along with a limited license to the Bally name and reintroduced it at Winter CES 1982. Astrovision units are otherwise the same except for the badging and are the most common Astrocades today, though the company became another victim of the 1983 video game crash and went bankrupt shortly afterwards. Simultaneously, an Astrocade derivative merged with Bally's unfinished ZGRASS-100 expansion chassis was reworked into the Datamax UV-1 graphics generator, sold in small numbers until around 1982.
