What does it mean to be a global remote team

2026-08-26

We’ve had a year full of growth and reached a milestone of 60+ team members; let’s take a moment to digest what that means for people operations. Hiring staff in 19+ countries is no easy task, but we are up for it, and I can honestly say it is the main reason why our culture is so great. That might sound biased coming from Crossref’s first Head of Human Resources, but hear me out.

One of our four strategic initiatives is to ‘foster a strong team—because reliable infrastructure needs committed people who contribute to and realise the vision, and thrive doing it.’ Prioritising the team isn’t a nice-to-have. It’s essential to our work.

Before 2025, Crossref did not need to grow the HR function beyond a single HR Manager. In 2024, we began expanding our workforce more globally and worked our way into a new organisational structure in an effort to become a more sustainable organisation that could meet the needs of our global community, well, forever. As we grew in all directions, it became clear that HR was evolving from mainly administrative to more strategic. We’ve had to tackle challenges around total rewards, policies, numerous local employment laws, systems, HR and payroll partners, leave management, and more.

So, what does it mean to be a global remote team? Great question. Let’s break it down into three categories: diversity, equity, and inclusion.

Diversity

My favourite one! Supporting a workforce so diverse is truly a joy. Think about it… bringing numerous cultures together to achieve our goals and strategic agenda is nothing short of phenomenal. As a Spanish-first speaker, I get to use my native language at work to support initiatives in Spanish-speaking countries. For example, I recently co-hosted our ¿Qué tan buenos son tus metadatos? webinar and supported our Ambassadors who did a live demo of our Metadata Manager tool. I even get to conduct new hire orientations in Spanish when the colleague is a native speaker too. We encourage our team members to make use of their known languages when possible. People with widely different backgrounds, intersectionalities, and career experiences are the main recipe for success. After all, we serve 25,000+ members from 167 countries, maintain 180+ million records, and handle 2 billion monthly metadata queries from thousands of tools across the research ecosystem.

This substantial growth in global membership and engagement pushed us to update our recruiting process. We used to collect applications via an email box, which was a time-consuming manual process. It was time to transition to an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) that could help us streamline our processes, manage applications, pipelines, and scorecards. We gathered feedback from hiring managers in order to create a consistent, fair, and unbiased recruitment process. Before going live with a job post, we need to do a lot of work. Once the job description is finalised, HR works with the hiring manager to develop the recruitment plan, including advertising, application questions, scorecard criteria, interview questions, and any exercises or assessments. For the last round of interviews, we share all questions and/or exercises with the candidates beforehand so everyone has the same opportunity and time to prepare. Research and HR guidance consistently frame this practice as a bias-reduction tool—when every candidate gets the same information and preparation time, the interview measures actual competence rather than who performs best under surprise conditions. As we strive to attract a diverse pool of candidates, this practice also supports inclusivity by considering diverse needs.

We work with an employer-of-record (EOR) partner in order to hire in countries where we do not have a locally registered entity. EORs are crucial to staying compliant with numerous payroll and tax regulations, benefits, and changing local employment laws. We need partners that can meet our budget needs and provide the visibility, reporting, and compliance support required to align with our internal controls and annual audits.

Leave management could no longer be a manual process. We needed a tool that could let us accommodate many different public holidays, carryover rules, seniority dates, and schedules while having reliable reporting and record-keeping of accruals. Vacation Tracker, our leave management platform, did a case study on the complexities of our diverse needs back in 2024.

On the training end, we rolled out two courses in 2024 that would nurture and enhance our multicultural workforce: unconscious bias and cultural competence. After completion of the courses, we ran an anonymous survey to collect feedback on their effectiveness and to reveal what areas needed more attention. We decided to make these two courses part of the new-hire onboarding process.

Equity

The most challenging part. How do you create equitable compensation, benefits, and policies across 19+ countries? We had to shift from local to globally minded approaches. As we are committed to meeting the POSI principles of transparent governance, we openly share our employee manual, policies and procedures. These detail our standards for compensation, benefits, paid time off, paid leaves, and others.

All countries have different statutory requirements for benefits. When statutory benefits are less than our standard offering, we will try to enhance the benefit, and when the benefits surpass our standards, we will have to plan and accommodate for them. All of our policies will run concurrently with local employment laws. Let’s look at an overview of:

Compensation: We went from location-based to location-agnostic compensation. This means we used to pay salaries based on local salary ranges and benchmarking, and now pay salaries based on internal salary ranges and benchmarking against global averages. For budgeting purposes, our internal salaries are USD-based, but we pay salaries in the currency of the country in which team members are based. We arrive at the local USD-equivalent salary by determining the average 5-year USD exchange rate, to stabilize currency fluctuations.

These changes require reliable data in the form of internal equity analytics and global salary benchmarking. We first analysed our internal equity when practicing a location-based approach and found that comp-ratios were consistent across teams and levels. This made the transition to a location-agnostic approach smoother. We’ve had to make adjustments to keep up with internal equity and also based on our annual average 5-year USD exchange analysis. After 3 years of practicing these principles, we are reassessing our compensation policy and it will be updated to reflect our growing needs.

Benefits: We have standards of what we offer our team members when it comes to medical, dental, vision, retirement, income protection, and life insurance benefits. Staff participate in these schemes either through statutory benefits or private premiums. These vary by location.

Paid Time Off: Most staff start with 25 days of PTO per year and add an extra day per anniversary year up to 30 days. In the US, staff start with 20 days of PTO per year, add 5 days at the 5th anniversary, and add another 5 days at the 10th anniversary, capping at 30 days. PTO is pro-rated based on start date and working hours per week. Public holidays are separate from PTO and from our unlimited sick time policy.

Paid family and medical leaves: Eligible employees receive up to 12 weeks of fully paid leave per benefit year—to bond with a new child within their first 12 months, or to care for their own or a family member’s serious health condition. Leaves vary by location and run concurrently with local entitlements.

As part of my 2026 goals, I am working on establishing HR controls that support our staffing operations across different countries, while maintaining the consistency needed to ensure equity and sustainability.

Inclusion

It’s how we work. If I could define our way of working in one word, I would go with inclusiveness. It takes a lot of intentionality to work this way and even more so in fully remote work environments. Systematically, we are inclusive by default when staffing across countries and maintaining globally minded policies. Culturally, we have to be intentional about how we work, communicate, and collaborate.

Having 13 different time zones with a spread of 12 hours has been a learning process. We’ve had to find out what days and UTC times work best for teams and all-staff meetings, sometimes accommodating multiple options or alternating between two times. We’ve come together and agreed on Slack best practices to help us navigate expectations around communication. One big area for us is being mindful of work-life balance and how we do not expect team members to reply or be available in Slack outside their local working hours. We also utilise Slack to stay connected in fun ways such as coffee-klatsches, quizzes, and channels on different topics. In our job adverts, we include a specific UTC time range as a required availability window for each role, which is dependent on the needs of each team. These are key aspects of working asynchronously.

In 2023, I facilitated our first psychological safety workshop and have continued to train our new hires on an annual basis. The workshop focuses on four areas: encouraging everyone to speak up, establishing norms for how we handle failure, creating space for new ideas, and embracing productive conflict. Together, we have defined what psychological safety means at Crossref. I love to advocate for it whenever I can as it is very impactful to our inclusive culture when we make it a priority. We added psychological safety sections to our annual development meetings and internal staff surveys.

We strive for cross-team collaboration, as every team member participates in at least one of our three program steering groups: Open and Sustainable Operations (OSO), Co-creation and Community Trends (CCT), and Contribute to the Research Nexus (CRN). Sharing ideas, contributions, updates, and project discussions across teams keeps everyone included, aligned, and informed. We also rolled out a new initiative to foster collaboration, curiosity, and connection between colleagues across different teams and levels in the organisation — the New Buddy and Curious Buddy programs.

We have partnered with training experts in areas that foster inclusiveness. CSCCE facilitated giving and receiving feedback workshops, and ICA:UK facilitated group facilitation methods and consensus-building workshops for team members in group-leading roles. This year, we have partnered with Caplor Horizons and are making cultural training a priority because of our growth spurt and the understanding that every team member contributes to our culture. This is a great opportunity to strengthen our inclusive culture and align on how we work.

In early 2026, Caplor Horizons conducted an anonymous cultural survey and internal interviews. After analysing the results, we planned for our first remote workshop and then a second workshop in person at our annual all-staff. This year, a record 53 people from 19 different countries attended the annual in-person all-staff in Ghent, Belgium. We plan all year for this event and put a lot of effort into making the most out of our time together with a one-week agenda that includes workshops, presentations, co-working time, birds of a feather (BoF) meetings, team lunches, dinners, city tours, a pub quiz, and other fun activities. This is the second year we have done a prioritisation session where everyone gets to vote on projects to prioritise for the following year by using strategic drivers as the baseline criteria — the results of this exercise are reflected in our roadmap. The projects are the result of a remote brainstorm session where everyone gets to participate and contribute.

Here you can see us hard at work—each dot represents a vote, and each colour represents a strategic driver. Team members who couldn’t make the in-person all-staff meeting could also vote remotely—all votes were accounted for!

We also enjoyed the strengths, trial-and-report, and team communication exercises facilitated by our Caplor Horizons trainers. We had a chance to show appreciation for our colleagues, gather feedback on our trial-and-report results, and reflect on the ways we communicate. The trial-and-report exercise was a result of our first remote workshop, where we all contributed ideas to have a positive impact in our main areas of improvement—our collective ideas turned into action as we tried new ways of working and tools for 8-10 weeks prior to the all-staff.

Here you can see us showing appreciation for each other during our strengths exercise—lots of happy faces.

So, what does it mean to be a global remote team?

Being a global remote team is not a checkbox but a living commitment that requires constant intention and care. Diversity, equity, and inclusion are the framework behind every policy we write, every hire we make, and every meeting we schedule across 13 time zones. Do we have it all figured out? Absolutely not. But we show up every day genuinely invested in getting it right — for our team members, for our members around the globe, and for the research community we serve. As we celebrate 60+ team members and look ahead, I am proud of how far we have come and even more excited about where we are going. The future looks bright — we are investing $4.9 million of our surplus in rebuilding the Crossref system and we will need people to help us get there. Be on the lookout for new job opportunities as we continue to grow.

Registering rich metadata for books, chapters, reports, dissertations, and updates just got easier

2026-08-20

You can now register rich metadata for a variety of record types in Metadata Manager using simple, guided workflows. The tool is used by many in our community to register grant and journal article records, and it has now been expanded to support books and chapters, reports, dissertations, and post-publication updates for journal content.

Metadata Manager makes it easier to capture identifiers for contributors and their affiliations, funding information, references, and more. Thanks to automated reference matching by Crossref, this offers greater visibility for the works, while the affiliations and funding information can serve as important trust signals for the community.

While all these record types have long been supported through our web deposit form, updated Metadata Manager simplifies the workflow, reduces complexity, and enables metadata updates for registered records. This gives our members the option to register these records along with rich metadata efficiently, without needing to work directly with XML files. We encourage existing web deposit form users to switch to Metadata Manager and the good news is that they can use their existing Crossref credentials to log in.

This is the latest step in expanding Metadata Manager to support more of the record types our members register daily. We retired the legacy Metadata Manager at the end of 2025 partly because it could not be extended to support other record types besides journal content. In contrast, we built the new Metadata Manager in a schema-driven way which makes it adaptable to all parts of our input schema, as well as any future schema changes.

And registering a DOI record doesn’t have to be the end of the story. Metadata Manager makes it easier to manage metadata after registration, allowing members to edit existing metadata directly in the helper tool—without having to re-enter the full record. Updating your records is free, and this new editability makes stewarding your metadata simpler.

In this update, our team focused on the record types members use most often. Our goal was to make DOI registration easier and more accessible.

We are currently working on adding support for registering conference proceedings and conference paper records in Metadata Manager; you can expect to see this next update in the coming weeks.

Why this update matters

Making it easier to register books and chapters, reports and working papers, dissertations, and other research outputs helps ensure more of the scholarly record is described with rich metadata. Better metadata strengthens the links between research outputs, contributors, organisations, grants, and other research objects, supporting the Research Nexus and making scholarly information easier to discover, cite, connect, and reuse. And given the importance of clarity about post-publication updates, such as corrections and retractions, for the integrity of the scholarly record, the Metadata Manager will better support our members to adopt industry best practices that enable self-correcting mechanisms in research.

The new functionality is available to use right away. If you’ve never used the new Metadata Manager, just log in to the tool with the same Crossref credentials you use elsewhere and start exploring the workflow.

To go far, go together: reflections from FORCE2026

2026-08-19

This year’s edition of the FORCE11 conference was hosted in June by Singapore Management University. Its title, “To Go Far, Go Together: Advancing Scholarly Communication Across Boundaries and Disruptions” truly resonates with Crossref’s truths; it takes a village! Crossref only works as long as the scholarly research community wants to work together globally, across all disciplines, for integrity and openness of the scientific record. The conversations were influenced by a genuinely diverse mix of librarians, researchers, open infrastructures, and service providers, and that mix shaped almost every interaction we had at the conference.

Two themes ran through nearly every session we attended: research assessment reform and research integrity. A third, quieter thread, a move towards more modular scholarship, kept surfacing as a possible answer to the complexities arising from the two previous themes. Here’s what stood out.

Research assessment reform

The pre-conference CoARA-DORA event set the tone before FORCE2026 had even officially started. Around 30 people including librarians, researchers, and funders, gathered for a day that made one thing clear: reforming research assessment is no longer a theoretical conversation happening in the policy space – it’s a global practice, rooted in local particularities.

We heard from leaders from CoARA and DORA about how these initiatives are supporting the community in translating policy ambitions into practice through guidelines and dedicated working groups. We heard from CLACSO, Queensland University of Technology, Kyoto University, and the Ministry for Business, Innovation and Employment of New Zealand how change needs to account for local contexts. It was also interesting to hear perspectives from policymakers, funders, and practitioners at universities on how the levers are different for each one.

This theme carried straight into the main conference. Lily Kong’s keynote, “From Publish or Perish to People and Purpose,” was blunt about the cost of the status quo: a damaged research culture, disincentivised collaboration, and researchers leaving academia altogether. Her argument wasn’t that we need better metrics, but that we need a different set of values: recognising impact as human capital development, community engagement, and a “full ecology of academic work,” where people and disciplines are allowed to shine under different lights rather than being measured against one standard.

Research integrity and AI

If assessment reform was a structural theme of the conference, AI was setting its anxious mood. The Day 2 keynote, “Surviving the Disruption: Scholarly Communication in the Age of AI,” captured a lot of what we’d been worrying about. AI is here to stay and it can improve much of what is important, but it also amplifies what is dangerous: poor quality information and integrity issues. The most important takeaway was that although we should be embracing the new status quo, humans need to stay central to the process.

This connected directly back to Lily Kong’s keynote, where she flagged AI as a force that could intensify, not solve, the pressures and distortions already present in research culture. Between the two keynotes, research integrity became a very concrete problem: how do we build systems, incentives, and review processes that hold up once AI is fully in the loop?

Modular scholarship: breaking out of the PDF

A persistent undertone was the push to move scholarship beyond the static, narrative-driven PDF. The session on Open Exchange Architecture made the case most directly: research is currently trapped in a “paper-shaped box,” with data, software, and code relegated to supplementary status, when they should be first-class, citable components in their own right.

At the funder forum, one discussion was entirely dedicated to integrating research software in open scholarship. An important aspect of this integration is how to ensure that these ‘components’ are discoverable from the moment they are created, interoperable, and findable. Not many people in the room were surprised when open metadata and persistent identifiers were described as a solution, and we agree.

Global perspectives on open research information

Together with Bianca Kramer (Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information) and Cameron Neylon (Curtin University), we organised a workshop “Global Perspectives on Open Research Information”. The workshop set out to explore why research information — the bibliographic metadata, metadata on research software and data, funding, and contributors — matters, and why it should be open. We made the case that closed data undermines fair assessment, equitable decision-making, and open science itself. We ran through the landscape of sources, and described Crossref infrastructure in more detail.

The session was participatory and interactive, we had attendees map their own use cases and data sources, followed by discussions and Q&A. Participants shared their own experiences with different sources, from CRIS systems and commercial databases to repositories, aggregators, and open databases. We discussed the value of the Crossref database as a large, open, rich, and community-governed database that 25,000 members contributed to and invested in.

The role of the community in open research infrastructure in Asia

To finalise, we had the opportunity to facilitate a panel on the accessibility of open scholarly infrastructure in Asia. We were joined by Mochammad Tanzil Multazam, from Relawan Journal Indonesia, Maria Lamury, from PKP, Xiaofeng Guo, Sin-Chn SCIENTIFIC PRESS and Crossref Ambassador, and Joanna Ball, from DOAJ, for an insightful conversation. As we explored what is needed for infrastructure to be accessible, one question kept coming up throughout the conversation: if communities can’t engage with it effectively, is it really open?

The role of community in skill building, supporting scholars in local languages, and sharing community feedback to the infrastructures was echoed by all panelists. This highlighted that open infrastructures need to support not only the technological layer, but the community layer too, which is fundamental for the accessibility of any infrastructure locally. This raised a question for infrastructure providers: what does it actually take to empower communities toward meaningful participation? At Crossref, we try to close that gap through our work with sponsors, ambassadors, and the GEM program (but there is always more to do!).

Closing thoughts

FORCE2026 didn’t resolve the tension between metrics and meaning, or between AI’s capabilities and our ability to harness them responsibly. But it did make clear that these conversations are maturing from principles and manifestos toward implementation. Reform looks different in Kyoto than in Queensland, and different again in Buenos Aires, and that’s not a bug, but a feature. The role of collaboration and community to realise a more open scholarly landscape can’t be overstated and we for one can’t wait to do our part.
