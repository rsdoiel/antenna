Investing $4.9 million of our surplus in rebuilding the Crossref system

2026-08-12

The infrastructure that registers and distributes Crossref metadata has been running and growing for more than two decades. It now does considerably more than it was built to do, and it shows: two large, tightly coupled monoliths (Content System and REST API), where a change in one place risks breaking something in another.

In its July meeting, the Crossref board approved the use of Crossref surplus funds for a three-year project to accelerate the redesign of the Crossref system. Our plan is to use $1.6 million a year for three years (~USD $4.9M in total) from our surplus funds, which will help us set up a modern infrastructure consisting of separate services for the different functionalities that the Crossref system offers.

This infusion of time-limited resources will augment our existing team. We will continue to maintain the current system, add small improvements, fix bugs, and make schema changes. We’ve mapped out our time and capacity to ensure that we can keep the current system active and robust while building the new system at the same time. In the past, when we’ve attempted large redesign initiatives, we’ve done a code freeze on the existing system—we won’t be doing that this time. 25,000+ members and thousands of downstream services rely on the system daily, so we will continue to provide time and energy to support that while building for the future. As always, you can view our public roadmap to see progress with what we’re working on.

Background

Over the years, the board, leadership team, and staff have discussed the need to address long-term technical debt in the Crossref system and the need to move from a monolithic codebase to a more modern, manageable, resilient technology that recreates the Crossref system. We are aiming to do this through a set of connected services and functions that better meet our mission and enable the Research Nexus, our goal of a rich and reusable open network of relationships connecting research organisations, people, things, and actions.

Our colleagues Paul Davis and Sara Bowman described some of the current challenges and plans earlier this year in Hit refresh: redesigning our technical infrastructure. We migrated from a closed-source database to PostgreSQL in 2024, moved out of a physical data centre into the cloud in August 2025, and have steadily been deprecating older tools not fit for purpose, such as Event Data, Co-access and ‘old’ Metadata Manager while rebuilding replacements. That was some of the groundwork; now we’re rebuilding the centre.

The two largest components of the current architecture are the Content System (CS) and the REST API. Both function as monoliths. They use mono-repositories, and their internal components are tightly coupled. Over time, they have accumulated complexity, substantially expanding beyond their original purpose. Each of them is now responsible for many different functions, with different needs and characteristics. This monolithic and tightly coupled architecture makes it difficult to maintain, scale, or expand our system. Adding new functionality often requires workarounds, which further increases complexity and adds technical debt.

As a not-for-profit, any surplus we generate has three possible destinations: reserves, paying down debt, or reinvestment in the mission. We met our goal of holding twelve months of operating costs in reserve in 2023, and we carry no debt, so reinvestment is where the rest belongs. We aim to generate surpluses to ensure that we are operating within our means, but we don’t need to accumulate them.

Some of that reinvestment goes outside Crossref; this year we’ve supported other community initiatives and infrastructure that people rely on, totalling around $500,000. A large proportion of the rest should go where members will feel it.

Reinvestment also has to be the right shape. Under the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure, time-limited funds are used only for time-limited activities: day-to-day operations are covered by day-to-day revenue, and one-off funds go to one-off projects. A three-year rebuild, staffed by fixed-term roles embedded in existing teams, meets that test.

What will we rebuild?

At the end of 2024, we introduced a new structure with Crossref work happening within three cross-functional programs: Co-creation and Community Trends (CCT), Contributing to the Research Nexus (CRN), and Open and Sustainable Operations (OSO). The redesign of the Crossref system will also happen within these three programs. The approach we’ve taken is that we’ve identified the different functionalities of the current system, and divided these between the three programs. In the tables below, you can see which services will be rebuilt within each program.

The three-year timeline refers to the duration of the additional investment that is now being made. We don’t expect all of the rebuild work to be complete within three years. And system maintenance and improvement is never done. The additional resources will accelerate our ability to address these projects and get them substantially complete or meaningfully underway within three years. We will start with the things that are easiest to separate out of the current system, and work our way through it. The deposit system will be the last part we’ll tackle— after the initial 3-year period. Exactly how we’ll break out or couple together its components will depend somewhat on how the rest of the work in all three programs proceeds.

Additionally, there will be work in the OSO program to pull out deprecated/unused code from the content system as other programs build new free-standing services.

Of course, an end goal is to rebuild all of this in open code repositories, and eventually turn our amber-coloured ‘open source’ to green in our biannual POSI audits.

What does this new investment and approach mean for our community?

Having seen the long list of things we’re planning to do, you may be wondering how this will impact you. Here are some things you should know:

You’ll be able to use our services throughout this project. Part of the reason why we are rebuilding different functions one by one is that we don’t want to interrupt the services we offer to our growing membership and users. The current services will continue to run until the new ones are in place, and we’ll have clear transition plans and documentation in cases where you need to update your workflows.

We won’t just rebuild like-for-like, but we’ll make improvements when we rebuild services. We want to continue to provide the functionality we currently offer to our members, but we also realise there are things that could be better. The clearest example of this is member self-service, which will be one of the main priorities for the CCT program next year. For example, as mentioned above, in 2025, about 15% of member support tickets were contact updates: changes members should be able to make themselves in seconds, but which our membership team handles by hand; that is the kind of thing that will improve life for both members and staff.

We are planning to deprecate some older/less-used services along the way, but we’ll give you plenty of notice. Crossref is probably running more services than you realise! We’re therefore evaluating all our services as part of the redesign, and keeping only the ones that are actively used and really add value for the community. If we decide to retire a service, we will contact all users so they have enough time to transition to an alternative service. Everything we’ve deprecated so far is also itemised on our website.

Community input will be a big part of the process. To ensure the services we offer meet your needs, we will need to keep talking with you. Our community managers and UX researchers will be reaching out to users of different services to understand use cases and ensure these continue to be met within the new system.

This blog is the first tagged ‘ system-redesign’ so follow this category for ongoing updates. If you have feedback or questions, please head to the dedicated community forum space for system redesign and ask us in the open.

Two halves of the same decision

Later this week we’ll also announce the latest round of fee changes resulting from our Resourcing Crossref for Future Sustainability (RCFS) work. What we charge, and what we do with what we collect, are two halves of the same decision; fees are to come down at the same time as we commit the largest single investment in our infrastructure we have ever made. Both follow from the same principle: we set fees to cover the cost of running the infrastructure, and when we hold more than we need, it goes back to the community and into the longevity of the scholarly record.

Our recent position paper on PIDs in research infrastructure policy argues that the value of an identifier lies in the metadata and services around it, and in whether those can be maintained over the long term. This approach to futureproofing open scholarly infrastructure is an example of that maintenance in action.

Share your feedback on the new Crossref Member Practices

2026-08-10

In December 2026, we plan to publish new Crossref Member Practices which will set out the standards we expect all Crossref members to meet. From August to October 2026 we are consulting with our members on a draft of these Member Practices. This blog provides the background to this, and explains how you can share your views.

In response to community concerns, in July 2025 our Board decided to update our Member Terms to require members to follow a broader set of Member Practices. As these are now drafted by a dedicated working group, we invite feedback on these draft Member Practices from all of our community by 9th of October 2026. Read on to learn more about the aims and development process of the Member Practices, and the different ways to offer your feedback.

Background on the development of Crossref Member Practices

Crossref membership is open to organisations that produce professional and scholarly materials and content. All members have agreed to our member terms, which outline both the benefits of membership and the responsibilities that come with it. We deliberately keep barriers to membership low, and we don’t assess the quality of our members’ outputs or the integrity of the research process as this isn’t part of our mission.

Instead, our role is to preserve the integrity of the scholarly record in a specific way. Our members can create and share Crossref DOIs – unique and persistent identifiers and metadata records for the objects they produce. These metadata records are openly shared through our free APIs, allowing the global scholarly community to discover, connect, and evaluate research.

Each DOI and metadata record confirms that an item has been published, provides a persistent link to the content, and identifies the organisation responsible for both the publication and the metadata. Members are encouraged to include rich information about their records—such as reference links, ORCID iDs for authors, ROR IDs for affiliations, funding information, licensing details, and other metadata—that provides evidence of their publishing practices and helps place their outputs within the scholarly record.

This information allows others to assess a publisher, its processes, and its content. A DOI itself is therefore not a mark of quality or endorsement - it simply confirms that an item has been published and provides metadata that can be considered alongside other trust signals when evaluating the content or the publisher. As we explained in our blog ISR part one: What is our role in preserving the integrity of the scholarly record?:

“Crossref is focused on enriching metadata to provide more and better trust signals while keeping barriers to membership and participation as low as possible to enable an inclusive scholarly record.”

Current member responsibilities

Our member terms have always required our members to keep their DOI records active, accurate, and up-to-date. Members are responsible for:

  • Registering accurate and timely DOIs and metadata records for the content they have legal rights to register.
  • Not registering records for content that they do not have legal rights to register.
  • Not registering duplicate DOI records for content that already has a DOI.
  • Not displaying DOI strings that have not been registered with Crossref.
  • Ensuring their DOIs continuously resolve to an active, publicly accessible landing page that displays the DOI following our display guidelines.
  • Updating metadata and posting a notice if content is withdrawn.
  • Promptly correcting any errors or inaccuracies in their metadata record communicated to them by Crossref.
  • Participating in Reference Linking by displaying their own and other members’ DOIs on any references provided in their own published content.

Why introduce additional Member Practices?

Over the past several years, conversations with the scholarly community have shown that people are interested not only in the metadata our members provide, but also in members’ broader practices. The community has continued to ask what Crossref can do when a member is not following responsible or ethical practices.

We also recognise that part of our role is helping members understand what it means to participate in the Crossref community. As we explained in ISR part two: How our membership approach helps to preserve the integrity of the scholarly record:

“Inclusivity is very important to us - after all, one of our organisational truths (the guiding principles for everything we do) is “come one, come all”, and this is mirrored in the POSI principles that commit us to broad stakeholder representation. We know that for new organisations, it may take them a while to be able to completely fulfil the membership obligations. We support them with information to help them understand what being a participant in the Crossref community entails. These organisations would have less of a chance of developing better practices if we were to limit membership in Crossref to ‘proven’ candidates.”

With this in mind, we decided to develop a set of Crossref Member Practices to clearly describe the expectations of membership. Our aim is to help our diverse members understand what responsible participation in the Crossref community looks like, regardless of organisation type, region of the world, or discipline. Additionally, where we see an ongoing pattern of non-compliance, particularly if a member does not make reasonable efforts to improve after concerns have been raised, this set of practices offers clear grounds for suspending or ultimately revoking their Crossref membership. We wanted to be clear that deceptive or fraudulent practices are not acceptable for Crossref members.

In July 2025, we updated our Member Terms to require members to follow new Member Practices. Since January 2026, we’ve been working in consultation with the Crossref Member Practices Working Group to develop the practices collaboratively. The working group brings together research integrity experts and scholarly sleuths at publishers, institutions, funders, and community organisations, including COPE and DOAJ. Together, they have shared different perspectives and challenges, helping to shape practical recommendations for the Member Practices. We would like to extend our thanks to this group for all of their suggestions and efforts over the past few months.

While these recommendations are mainly based on the most common issues that have been brought to our notice by the community and largely come from journal publishing, the Member Practices are meant to be adhered to by all Crossref members. These Member Practices will also be reviewed annually with the aim of updating them in line with trends that we notice in the community.

Throughout the group’s discussions, the group has also been clear about what will not change with the introduction of the Member Practices.

What hasn’t changed?

We are still unable to get involved in individual instances of non-compliant activity, we don’t intervene in legal or financial disputes between parties, and we don’t assess the quality of published content or research. But where we see compelling evidence of deceptive or fraudulent activities, where there are systematic problems indicating that a member is no longer acting responsibly, or particularly if a member does not make reasonable efforts to improve after being notified of concerns, we may suspend or ultimately revoke their Crossref membership.

Share your feedback on the draft Member Practices

We invite you to read the draft Member Practices and share your feedback with us. We are keen to understand from our members whether these practices feel realistic and achievable, whether they exclude any part of the community, and if there is anything missing that needs to be included in the Member Practices. As you read the Member Practices, we encourage you to consider:

  • Are the outlined Member Practices in line with your expectations of standard best publishing practices? Is there anything that surprised you?
  • Do the outlined Crossref Member Practices feel realistic and achievable? Are there any barriers to fulfilling them?
  • Are there any behaviours that have been discouraged in the Member Practices but are considered a standard practice in your community?
  • Is there anything missing that you would like to see included in the Member Practices?
  • Do you need any more information or guidance that would help you in fulfilling any of the Member Practices?

There are several ways in which you can provide your feedback on the draft:

  • Come to one of our drop-in webinars where you can ask any questions that you may have about the new Member Practices. Register for the webinars via the below:
    • 25th August at 1300 UTC
    • 15th September at 1700 UTC
    • 24th September at 0700 UTC
  • Download the PDF of the Member Practices, add your comments in the document, and email this document to feedback@crossref.org with the subject line “Feedback on Crossref Member Practices”.
  • Email your feedback directly in an email to feedback@crossref.org with the subject line “Feedback on Crossref Member Practices”.
  • Share your thoughts on the community forum.
  • Discuss your feedback with us at one of the upcoming community events – you can find out where Crossref colleagues will be on hand at our events page (at the ‘Crossref attending’ section).

We also plan to organise online meetings with our members representing different areas and different publishing models and output types, so look out for an invitation to those.

The final date for sharing your feedback is 9th October.

Your feedback will be used to finalise the draft of the Member Practices and we will discuss it in our Annual Meeting on 22nd of October. The finalised set of Member Practices will then be shared with the Crossref board for a final review and approval.

Building Trust through Metadata: a recap of the Crossref learning series

2026-08-06

The Crossref community in India is as diverse as the region that it represents, comprising 1605 members, 11 sponsoring organisations, and 5 ambassadors, who between them cover the length and breadth of this country. Between November 2025 and March 2026, we organised three webinars focused on supporting this community with best metadata and publishing practices. We collaborated with the Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ) and the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) to embed understanding of metadata’s role in the greater context of publishing integrity.

Together, the webinars explored current best publishing practices, the role played by rich metadata and Crossref’s infrastructure in enabling these, and how members can make the most of their Crossref membership. As the topics we covered are relevant to all Crossref members – this blog presents a summary of the sessions and key themes that arose from the discussions. You can also access the slides and recordings of all these sessions.

Publishing best practices and metadata: Crossref essentials + DOAJ

The series began on 13th November 2025, with a refresher on our vision, mission, the basics of our infrastructure, an overview from DOAJ about their indexing criteria, and ethics in open access publishing.

Dr. Sushil Kumar, Crossref ambassador in India, reminded the audience about Crossref’s mission, our Research Nexus vision, and how our members contribute to this shared infrastructure when they assign DOIs and submit rich metadata with their records. He also carefully explained that Crossref is not only about DOIs and DOIs themselves do not say anything about the quality of a publication. Rather, a DOI is a persistent identifier for an item, which signals that the item exists. He also pointed to the steps to keep in mind when getting started with content registration, which include choosing a content registration method and deciding how to construct your DOI suffixes.

As Crossref members, in addition to registering unique DOIs for objects you publish or share, you also provide rich metadata for these records. Maryna Kovalyova, Member Experience Manager at Crossref, highlighted why this metadata matters and the key elements that make your records rich. She showcased the Crossref Participation Reports, which is a free tool that allows you to visualize what percentage of your metadata records contain the 11 key metadata elements. Importantly, the “Download Gap Report” feature of the tool enables the download of a list of DOIs that are missing a certain metadata element.

We then heard from Dr. Vrushali Dandawate, DOAJ Senior Ambassador Asia, who provided an overview of DOAJ which is a fellow open scholarly infrastructure that indexes open access journals. Vrushali spoke about the basic criteria for journals to be included in DOAJ, such as the requirements for journals to be fully open access, and the information that should be available about the applicant journals. As part of her presentation, Vrushali drew attention to the best publishing practices in open-access publishing, one of which is providing metadata such as author information, ORCID iDs, abstracts, and more, for promoting discoverability.

You can access the recording and all the slides from this webinar here.

Upholding integrity: Crossref member obligations and COPE guidance

The journey continued in 2026 with the second webinar of the series on January 21st. Amanda Bartell, Director of Membership at Crossref, kicked it off with a presentation on the role of Crossref in preserving the integrity of the scholarly record (ISR). As she had laid out in her earlier blog about ISR, “Crossref is focused on enriching metadata to provide more and better trust signals while keeping barriers to membership and participation as low as possible to enable an inclusive scholarly record”. There are a few different ways in which members can demonstrate their commitment towards preserving the integrity of the scholarly record. All Crossref members have to comply with member obligations such as:

  • depositing accurate metadata,
  • creating DOI links in a timely fashion,
  • registering only the content that a member has the legal right to,
  • making sure that there is a unique and active landing page for each DOI,
  • following display guidelines, and
  • maintaining and updating their metadata records, and linking their references.

Following these obligations not only keeps our members “in good standing”, but also allows them to demonstrate their support for preserving the integrity of the scholarly record by providing “trust signals” about the work that they publish. Amanda called out some key metadata elements that are particularly important as trust signals. These include references, links to data, contributors and their roles, including ORCID iDs, affiliations and affiliation identifiers (ROR IDs), and funding information. This metadata is used by the wider scholarly community as signals about the trustworthiness of the item, since they provide important context about the work such as which organisations and people contributed to it and what was their contribution, who funded the work, whether the work was checked for originality, whether it was updated after publication, and more.

Practices such as displaying DOI strings that are not registered, DOIs resolving to a 404 page, and metadata containing inaccuracies, can lead to loss of trust in the member’s commitment. This can be avoided by ensuring that all DOIs are always registered by the member, by updating resolution URLs when content moves, and by registering accurate metadata.

Good metadata matters.

The best way to demonstrate your commitment towards upholding the scholarly record is by registering working DOIs, depositing rich and accurate metadata about your content, and making updates to existing metadata when required.

Another way to demonstrate trust is by openly managing any updates that happen to your content post its publication. If an item published by you has been withdrawn or retracted, the best practice is to update its metadata record by adding the word “withdrawn” or “retracted” (as the case may be) to the article’s title, and adding the retraction or withdrawal metadata in the Crossmark section of our schema.

COPE’s retraction guidelines also include the importance of metadata in accurately capturing retractions in the scholarly record, added Dr. Marie Souliere, COPE council member and our guest speaker at this webinar. She shared the latest COPE guidance around retractions and expressions of concern. Some of the advice covered by these guidelines include when to consider retracting articles and when to issue expressions of concern, what should be included in these notices, and handling communication with authors. Marie emphasized that retractions are not a mechanism for punishing the publication’s authors but rather are essential for correcting literature and maintaining the integrity of the scholarly record. COPE guidance also recommends capturing bidirectional links between the article and the associated expression of concern and also linking the retraction notice to the retracted article.

The recording of the second webinar and all the slides from both the speakers are available here.

Preserving the scholarly record: how to deposit post publication updates with Crossref

The culminating webinar in this learning series was held in March 2026 and was a refresher on the different ways in which Crossref members can deposit any post-publication updates to their content. We first discussed how retractions and corrections are significant changes to a content’s status and it’s important to capture them accurately in the scholarly record, so that readers are always citing and reading the most up to date information. Although whether content needs to be updated and what type of update is warranted are usually editorial decisions, recording this update in the scholarly record is the publisher’s responsibility and it goes a long way in maintaining the accuracy of the scholarly record.

Shayn Smulyan, Technical Support Specialist at Crossref, then provided a comprehensive overview of metadata related to post publication updates. The basics to manage post-publication updates openly are:

  • if content has been retracted, withdrawn or corrected, do not just remove the page from your website,
  • set the content’s DOI to resolve to a notification page that explains the update, and
  • update the metadata record to indicate the update.

Numerous types of post-publication updates are supported by our schema and the most common ones are correction, erratum, retraction, withdrawal, corrigendum, expression of concern, and withdrawal. There are multiple options for sending these through: if you are comfortable with writing XML files, you can submit updates via XML deposit.

Although updates are registered in the Crossmark part of the schema, you do not need to be a Crossmark user to deposit your updates. We have several examples of how to register updates in our documentation that you can refer to. These include examples on how to link retraction or correction notices with the original item.

The web deposit form, which is one of our helper tools for content registration, can also be used for depositing updates. However, via this method you will need to re-enter and submit all the metadata for your record, not just the metadata related to the update.

Shayn also touched on the Crossmark service. We are aware that this service has been technically challenging for our members to implement but as mentioned previously, you do not need to be a Crossmark user to deposit metadata on post publication updates. The main benefit of implementing Crossmark is that it helps to surface information about post-publication updates for readers in a consistent manner, even if they are accessing PDFs of publications away from the publisher’s website. Those interested in implementing the service need to publish a Crossmark policy page and register a DOI for it.

Detailed information on doing this is available here.

You can now also use our newest helper tool, the Metadata Manager to register post-publication updates for your journal content. To do this, choose the “post-publication update” option in the Metadata Manager and then fill it out with metadata of the update notice and the DOI of the journal content that is being updated. This might be a suitable option for those of you who are using OJS for sending your metadata to Crossref.

The recording and slides from this webinar can be accessed here.

Questions from the community

We had a very engaged audience for these webinars who had a lot of questions for our speakers. We thought it might be helpful to share the answers to some of them here:

How do I register my content?

You need to join Crossref as a member to register your objects with us (those could be journal articles, book chapters, dissertations, conference proceedings, datasets, grants, and more). Once you join – you will receive a DOI prefix. You can combine this DOI prefix with a suffix of your choice to create a DOI. Content registration enables you to register the DOI and deposit or update the metadata associated with the DOI record. The metadata about your content is stored with us as XML. If you are comfortable with writing XML, you can send us your XML files directly. But if you are not comfortable with that, you can use any one of our three helper tools. You can take a look at our quick guide for choosing your content registration method to learn more.

How to get a DOI for a journal published by a not-for-profit society?

The process for membership remains the same for everyone: simply apply here to join Crossref.

What is the use of XML files?

All of the metadata that is deposited with Crossref is submitted as XML. You can either submit the XML files yourself directly or you can use one of the helper tools to enter your metadata, which is then converted into XML and deposited with Crossref for you.

Has Crossref come across spam DOIs that mimic Crossref DOIs?

Take a look at this ticket of the month that my colleague from the member support team, Collin Knopp-Schwyn put together, on how to identify strings that look like DOIs but are just a hyperlink.

In many disciplines, authors frequently cite scientifically legitimate sources that do not have DOIs, such as institutional reports, public policy documents, and technical standards. Although these are valid and essential from a scholarly perspective, their citations are not incorporated into Crossref's citation linking. Is Crossref planning any future developments to better recognise these legitimate non-DOI references?

It is correct that not all references may have a DOI but it is still important to include such references in your metadata record. Our system regularly checks to see if a DOI has been registered for the cited item and if so, matches the reference to the DOI. Registering these references also means downstream systems that use Crossref metadata can ingest these references and use them for any citation linking they may be performing.

What is Crossmark and how is it connected or integrated with Crossref services?

Crossmark provides a way for readers to consistently discover the status of a research output, along with additional metadata related to the editorial process. You do not need to implement the full Crossmark service to deliver post publication update metadata. If you are interested in using the service, more information on how to participate is available in our documentation.

What are the best practices to report retractions in DOI metadata?

Take a look at this ticket of the month that my colleague from the technical support team, Kathleen Luschek, wrote, detailing why retractions are important, how to register them, and how to retrieve them.

Can you indicate an update to an article via Crossmark for a small correction that might not warrant a Correction?

When a change is made to a document without issuing a separate update or correction, it is called an in-situ update. In-situ updates are not recommended because they can obscure the scholarly record. However, sometimes members might need to support such updates and we have documentation available on how to do this.

Is there a mechanism that allows integrity-related events to be linked when they occur within the same scholarly context (for example at the level of a publication or a journal)?

When a registered item is updated, the update is captured in the item’s metadata. If there are a series of updates, they are also captured in the item’s metadata. All of this metadata is openly available via Crossref’s APIs. Anyone in the community can then use this metadata to analyse it at a scale that they are interested in, for example at a journal or publication level.

Does Crossref make any effort to get organizations into ROR?

Anybody can submit a request for getting an organisation added to ROR via this form. The ROR team then reviews the request based on their scope and criteria for inclusion.

If you have any feedback, questions, or simply suggestions for other relevant topics that you would like to hear more about, I encourage you to post your thoughts on our community forum.
