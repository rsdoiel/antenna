What does it mean to be a global remote team

2026-08-26

We’ve had a year full of growth and reached a milestone of 60+ team members; let’s take a moment to digest what that means for people operations. Hiring staff in 19+ countries is no easy task, but we are up for it, and I can honestly say it is the main reason why our culture is so great. That might sound biased coming from Crossref’s first Head of Human Resources, but hear me out.

One of our four strategic initiatives is to ‘foster a strong team—because reliable infrastructure needs committed people who contribute to and realise the vision, and thrive doing it.’ Prioritising the team isn’t a nice-to-have. It’s essential to our work.

Before 2025, Crossref did not need to grow the HR function beyond a single HR Manager. In 2024, we began expanding our workforce more globally and worked our way into a new organisational structure in an effort to become a more sustainable organisation that could meet the needs of our global community, well, forever. As we grew in all directions, it became clear that HR was evolving from mainly administrative to more strategic. We’ve had to tackle challenges around total rewards, policies, numerous local employment laws, systems, HR and payroll partners, leave management, and more.

So, what does it mean to be a global remote team? Great question. Let’s break it down into three categories: diversity, equity, and inclusion.

Diversity

My favourite one! Supporting a workforce so diverse is truly a joy. Think about it… bringing numerous cultures together to achieve our goals and strategic agenda is nothing short of phenomenal. As a Spanish-first speaker, I get to use my native language at work to support initiatives in Spanish-speaking countries. For example, I recently co-hosted our ¿Qué tan buenos son tus metadatos? webinar and supported our Ambassadors who did a live demo of our Metadata Manager tool. I even get to conduct new hire orientations in Spanish when the colleague is a native speaker too. We encourage our team members to make use of their known languages when possible. People with widely different backgrounds, intersectionalities, and career experiences are the main recipe for success. After all, we serve 25,000+ members from 167 countries, maintain 180+ million records, and handle 2 billion monthly metadata queries from thousands of tools across the research ecosystem.

This substantial growth in global membership and engagement pushed us to update our recruiting process. We used to collect applications via an email box, which was a time-consuming manual process. It was time to transition to an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) that could help us streamline our processes, manage applications, pipelines, and scorecards. We gathered feedback from hiring managers in order to create a consistent, fair, and unbiased recruitment process. Before going live with a job post, we need to do a lot of work. Once the job description is finalised, HR works with the hiring manager to develop the recruitment plan, including advertising, application questions, scorecard criteria, interview questions, and any exercises or assessments. For the last round of interviews, we share all questions and/or exercises with the candidates beforehand so everyone has the same opportunity and time to prepare. Research and HR guidance consistently frame this practice as a bias-reduction tool—when every candidate gets the same information and preparation time, the interview measures actual competence rather than who performs best under surprise conditions. As we strive to attract a diverse pool of candidates, this practice also supports inclusivity by considering diverse needs.

We work with an employer-of-record (EOR) partner in order to hire in countries where we do not have a locally registered entity. EORs are crucial to staying compliant with numerous payroll and tax regulations, benefits, and changing local employment laws. We need partners that can meet our budget needs and provide the visibility, reporting, and compliance support required to align with our internal controls and annual audits.

Leave management could no longer be a manual process. We needed a tool that could let us accommodate many different public holidays, carryover rules, seniority dates, and schedules while having reliable reporting and record-keeping of accruals. Vacation Tracker, our leave management platform, did a case study on the complexities of our diverse needs back in 2024.

On the training end, we rolled out two courses in 2024 that would nurture and enhance our multicultural workforce: unconscious bias and cultural competence. After completion of the courses, we ran an anonymous survey to collect feedback on their effectiveness and to reveal what areas needed more attention. We decided to make these two courses part of the new-hire onboarding process.

Equity

The most challenging part. How do you create equitable compensation, benefits, and policies across 19+ countries? We had to shift from local to globally minded approaches. As we are committed to meeting the POSI principles of transparent governance, we openly share our employee manual, policies and procedures. These detail our standards for compensation, benefits, paid time off, paid leaves, and others.

All countries have different statutory requirements for benefits. When statutory benefits are less than our standard offering, we will try to enhance the benefit, and when the benefits surpass our standards, we will have to plan and accommodate for them. All of our policies will run concurrently with local employment laws. Let’s look at an overview of:

Compensation: We went from location-based to location-agnostic compensation. This means we used to pay salaries based on local salary ranges and benchmarking, and now pay salaries based on internal salary ranges and benchmarking against global averages. For budgeting purposes, our internal salaries are USD-based, but we pay salaries in the currency of the country in which team members are based. We arrive at the local USD-equivalent salary by determining the average 5-year USD exchange rate, to stabilize currency fluctuations.

These changes require reliable data in the form of internal equity analytics and global salary benchmarking. We first analysed our internal equity when practicing a location-based approach and found that comp-ratios were consistent across teams and levels. This made the transition to a location-agnostic approach smoother. We’ve had to make adjustments to keep up with internal equity and also based on our annual average 5-year USD exchange analysis. After 3 years of practicing these principles, we are reassessing our compensation policy and it will be updated to reflect our growing needs.

Benefits: We have standards of what we offer our team members when it comes to medical, dental, vision, retirement, income protection, and life insurance benefits. Staff participate in these schemes either through statutory benefits or private premiums. These vary by location.

Paid Time Off: Most staff start with 25 days of PTO per year and add an extra day per anniversary year up to 30 days. In the US, staff start with 20 days of PTO per year, add 5 days at the 5th anniversary, and add another 5 days at the 10th anniversary, capping at 30 days. PTO is pro-rated based on start date and working hours per week. Public holidays are separate from PTO and from our unlimited sick time policy.

Paid family and medical leaves: Eligible employees receive up to 12 weeks of fully paid leave per benefit year—to bond with a new child within their first 12 months, or to care for their own or a family member’s serious health condition. Leaves vary by location and run concurrently with local entitlements.

As part of my 2026 goals, I am working on establishing HR controls that support our staffing operations across different countries, while maintaining the consistency needed to ensure equity and sustainability.

Inclusion

It’s how we work. If I could define our way of working in one word, I would go with inclusiveness. It takes a lot of intentionality to work this way and even more so in fully remote work environments. Systematically, we are inclusive by default when staffing across countries and maintaining globally minded policies. Culturally, we have to be intentional about how we work, communicate, and collaborate.

Having 13 different time zones with a spread of 12 hours has been a learning process. We’ve had to find out what days and UTC times work best for teams and all-staff meetings, sometimes accommodating multiple options or alternating between two times. We’ve come together and agreed on Slack best practices to help us navigate expectations around communication. One big area for us is being mindful of work-life balance and how we do not expect team members to reply or be available in Slack outside their local working hours. We also utilise Slack to stay connected in fun ways such as coffee-klatsches, quizzes, and channels on different topics. In our job adverts, we include a specific UTC time range as a required availability window for each role, which is dependent on the needs of each team. These are key aspects of working asynchronously.

In 2023, I facilitated our first psychological safety workshop and have continued to train our new hires on an annual basis. The workshop focuses on four areas: encouraging everyone to speak up, establishing norms for how we handle failure, creating space for new ideas, and embracing productive conflict. Together, we have defined what psychological safety means at Crossref. I love to advocate for it whenever I can as it is very impactful to our inclusive culture when we make it a priority. We added psychological safety sections to our annual development meetings and internal staff surveys.

We strive for cross-team collaboration, as every team member participates in at least one of our three program steering groups: Open and Sustainable Operations (OSO), Co-creation and Community Trends (CCT), and Contribute to the Research Nexus (CRN). Sharing ideas, contributions, updates, and project discussions across teams keeps everyone included, aligned, and informed. We also rolled out a new initiative to foster collaboration, curiosity, and connection between colleagues across different teams and levels in the organisation — the New Buddy and Curious Buddy programs.

We have partnered with training experts in areas that foster inclusiveness. CSCCE facilitated giving and receiving feedback workshops, and ICA:UK facilitated group facilitation methods and consensus-building workshops for team members in group-leading roles. This year, we have partnered with Caplor Horizons and are making cultural training a priority because of our growth spurt and the understanding that every team member contributes to our culture. This is a great opportunity to strengthen our inclusive culture and align on how we work.

In early 2026, Caplor Horizons conducted an anonymous cultural survey and internal interviews. After analysing the results, we planned for our first remote workshop and then a second workshop in person at our annual all-staff. This year, a record 53 people from 19 different countries attended the annual in-person all-staff in Ghent, Belgium. We plan all year for this event and put a lot of effort into making the most out of our time together with a one-week agenda that includes workshops, presentations, co-working time, birds of a feather (BoF) meetings, team lunches, dinners, city tours, a pub quiz, and other fun activities. This is the second year we have done a prioritisation session where everyone gets to vote on projects to prioritise for the following year by using strategic drivers as the baseline criteria — the results of this exercise are reflected in our roadmap. The projects are the result of a remote brainstorm session where everyone gets to participate and contribute.

Here you can see us hard at work—each dot represents a vote, and each colour represents a strategic driver. Team members who couldn’t make the in-person all-staff meeting could also vote remotely—all votes were accounted for!

We also enjoyed the strengths, trial-and-report, and team communication exercises facilitated by our Caplor Horizons trainers. We had a chance to show appreciation for our colleagues, gather feedback on our trial-and-report results, and reflect on the ways we communicate. The trial-and-report exercise was a result of our first remote workshop, where we all contributed ideas to have a positive impact in our main areas of improvement—our collective ideas turned into action as we tried new ways of working and tools for 8-10 weeks prior to the all-staff.

Here you can see us showing appreciation for each other during our strengths exercise—lots of happy faces.

So, what does it mean to be a global remote team?

Being a global remote team is not a checkbox but a living commitment that requires constant intention and care. Diversity, equity, and inclusion are the framework behind every policy we write, every hire we make, and every meeting we schedule across 13 time zones. Do we have it all figured out? Absolutely not. But we show up every day genuinely invested in getting it right — for our team members, for our members around the globe, and for the research community we serve. As we celebrate 60+ team members and look ahead, I am proud of how far we have come and even more excited about where we are going. The future looks bright — we are investing $4.9 million of our surplus in rebuilding the Crossref system and we will need people to help us get there. Be on the lookout for new job opportunities as we continue to grow.

Registering rich metadata for books, chapters, reports, dissertations, and updates just got easier

2026-08-20

You can now register rich metadata for a variety of record types in Metadata Manager using simple, guided workflows. The tool is used by many in our community to register grant and journal article records, and it has now been expanded to support books and chapters, reports, dissertations, and post-publication updates for journal content.

Metadata Manager makes it easier to capture identifiers for contributors and their affiliations, funding information, references, and more. Thanks to automated reference matching by Crossref, this offers greater visibility for the works, while the affiliations and funding information can serve as important trust signals for the community.

While all these record types have long been supported through our web deposit form, updated Metadata Manager simplifies the workflow, reduces complexity, and enables metadata updates for registered records. This gives our members the option to register these records along with rich metadata efficiently, without needing to work directly with XML files. We encourage existing web deposit form users to switch to Metadata Manager and the good news is that they can use their existing Crossref credentials to log in.

This is the latest step in expanding Metadata Manager to support more of the record types our members register daily. We retired the legacy Metadata Manager at the end of 2025 partly because it could not be extended to support other record types besides journal content. In contrast, we built the new Metadata Manager in a schema-driven way which makes it adaptable to all parts of our input schema, as well as any future schema changes.

And registering a DOI record doesn’t have to be the end of the story. Metadata Manager makes it easier to manage metadata after registration, allowing members to edit existing metadata directly in the helper tool—without having to re-enter the full record. Updating your records is free, and this new editability makes stewarding your metadata simpler.

In this update, our team focused on the record types members use most often. Our goal was to make DOI registration easier and more accessible.

We are currently working on adding support for registering conference proceedings and conference paper records in Metadata Manager; you can expect to see this next update in the coming weeks.

Why this update matters

Making it easier to register books and chapters, reports and working papers, dissertations, and other research outputs helps ensure more of the scholarly record is described with rich metadata. Better metadata strengthens the links between research outputs, contributors, organisations, grants, and other research objects, supporting the Research Nexus and making scholarly information easier to discover, cite, connect, and reuse. And given the importance of clarity about post-publication updates, such as corrections and retractions, for the integrity of the scholarly record, the Metadata Manager will better support our members to adopt industry best practices that enable self-correcting mechanisms in research.

The new functionality is available to use right away. If you’ve never used the new Metadata Manager, just log in to the tool with the same Crossref credentials you use elsewhere and start exploring the workflow.

To go far, go together: reflections from FORCE2026

2026-08-19

This year’s edition of the FORCE11 conference was hosted in June by Singapore Management University. Its title, “To Go Far, Go Together: Advancing Scholarly Communication Across Boundaries and Disruptions” truly resonates with Crossref’s truths; it takes a village! Crossref only works as long as the scholarly research community wants to work together globally, across all disciplines, for integrity and openness of the scientific record. The conversations were influenced by a genuinely diverse mix of librarians, researchers, open infrastructures, and service providers, and that mix shaped almost every interaction we had at the conference.

Two themes ran through nearly every session we attended: research assessment reform and research integrity. A third, quieter thread, a move towards more modular scholarship, kept surfacing as a possible answer to the complexities arising from the two previous themes. Here’s what stood out.

Research assessment reform

The pre-conference CoARA-DORA event set the tone before FORCE2026 had even officially started. Around 30 people including librarians, researchers, and funders, gathered for a day that made one thing clear: reforming research assessment is no longer a theoretical conversation happening in the policy space – it’s a global practice, rooted in local particularities.

We heard from leaders from CoARA and DORA about how these initiatives are supporting the community in translating policy ambitions into practice through guidelines and dedicated working groups. We heard from CLACSO, Queensland University of Technology, Kyoto University, and the Ministry for Business, Innovation and Employment of New Zealand how change needs to account for local contexts. It was also interesting to hear perspectives from policymakers, funders, and practitioners at universities on how the levers are different for each one.

This theme carried straight into the main conference. Lily Kong’s keynote, “From Publish or Perish to People and Purpose,” was blunt about the cost of the status quo: a damaged research culture, disincentivised collaboration, and researchers leaving academia altogether. Her argument wasn’t that we need better metrics, but that we need a different set of values: recognising impact as human capital development, community engagement, and a “full ecology of academic work,” where people and disciplines are allowed to shine under different lights rather than being measured against one standard.

Research integrity and AI

If assessment reform was a structural theme of the conference, AI was setting its anxious mood. The Day 2 keynote, “Surviving the Disruption: Scholarly Communication in the Age of AI,” captured a lot of what we’d been worrying about. AI is here to stay and it can improve much of what is important, but it also amplifies what is dangerous: poor quality information and integrity issues. The most important takeaway was that although we should be embracing the new status quo, humans need to stay central to the process.

This connected directly back to Lily Kong’s keynote, where she flagged AI as a force that could intensify, not solve, the pressures and distortions already present in research culture. Between the two keynotes, research integrity became a very concrete problem: how do we build systems, incentives, and review processes that hold up once AI is fully in the loop?

Modular scholarship: breaking out of the PDF

A persistent undertone was the push to move scholarship beyond the static, narrative-driven PDF. The session on Open Exchange Architecture made the case most directly: research is currently trapped in a “paper-shaped box,” with data, software, and code relegated to supplementary status, when they should be first-class, citable components in their own right.

At the funder forum, one discussion was entirely dedicated to integrating research software in open scholarship. An important aspect of this integration is how to ensure that these ‘components’ are discoverable from the moment they are created, interoperable, and findable. Not many people in the room were surprised when open metadata and persistent identifiers were described as a solution, and we agree.

Global perspectives on open research information

Together with Bianca Kramer (Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information) and Cameron Neylon (Curtin University), we organised a workshop “Global Perspectives on Open Research Information”. The workshop set out to explore why research information — the bibliographic metadata, metadata on research software and data, funding, and contributors — matters, and why it should be open. We made the case that closed data undermines fair assessment, equitable decision-making, and open science itself. We ran through the landscape of sources, and described Crossref infrastructure in more detail.

The session was participatory and interactive, we had attendees map their own use cases and data sources, followed by discussions and Q&A. Participants shared their own experiences with different sources, from CRIS systems and commercial databases to repositories, aggregators, and open databases. We discussed the value of the Crossref database as a large, open, rich, and community-governed database that 25,000 members contributed to and invested in.

The role of the community in open research infrastructure in Asia

To finalise, we had the opportunity to facilitate a panel on the accessibility of open scholarly infrastructure in Asia. We were joined by Mochammad Tanzil Multazam, from Relawan Journal Indonesia, Maria Lamury, from PKP, Xiaofeng Guo, Sin-Chn SCIENTIFIC PRESS and Crossref Ambassador, and Joanna Ball, from DOAJ, for an insightful conversation. As we explored what is needed for infrastructure to be accessible, one question kept coming up throughout the conversation: if communities can’t engage with it effectively, is it really open?

The role of community in skill building, supporting scholars in local languages, and sharing community feedback to the infrastructures was echoed by all panelists. This highlighted that open infrastructures need to support not only the technological layer, but the community layer too, which is fundamental for the accessibility of any infrastructure locally. This raised a question for infrastructure providers: what does it actually take to empower communities toward meaningful participation? At Crossref, we try to close that gap through our work with sponsors, ambassadors, and the GEM program (but there is always more to do!).

Closing thoughts

FORCE2026 didn’t resolve the tension between metrics and meaning, or between AI’s capabilities and our ability to harness them responsibly. But it did make clear that these conversations are maturing from principles and manifestos toward implementation. Reform looks different in Kyoto than in Queensland, and different again in Buenos Aires, and that’s not a bug, but a feature. The role of collaboration and community to realise a more open scholarly landscape can’t be overstated and we for one can’t wait to do our part.

Fee update: lowering and removing DOI registration fees in January 2027

2026-08-13

We have some rather unusual news to share: Crossref fees are going down again. At its meeting in Paris earlier this month, the Crossref Board approved two further recommendations from the Resourcing Crossref for Future Sustainability (RCFS) project: effective 1st January 2027, we will reduce the majority of Content Registration fees for all 25,000 members, and remove fees for back-year records altogether.

Content Registration fees haven’t increased in around 20 years, and this is the first significant change to them in that time—downwards. Since these fees account for the largest share of Crossref’s revenue (around 60%), and since every member registers DOI records, these changes reach further than anything we’ve announced under the RCFS project so far.

What’s changing

1. Most Content Registration fees are reduced from January 2027. We’re simplifying record fees to just a few price points: most records currently priced at USD 1.00—journal articles, conference papers, book titles, dissertations, working papers, grants—will move to USD 0.95, and records at USD 0.25—book chapters, posted content (including preprints), and first peer reviews—will move to USD 0.20. Components and datasets stay at USD 0.06. One fee goes up slightly in the name of simplification: standards move from USD 0.15 to USD 0.20.

Simplification isn’t an end in itself, either: fewer price points take us towards one of our stated goals: making fees more predictable. Simplifying our fees from 16 different prices down to three means organisations can more easily estimate their costs and will allow us to offer a fee calculator in the future.

2. All fees for back-year records are removed. Back-year records are works produced more than two full years ago (so in 2027, anything published in 2024 or earlier). Starting in January 2027, registering them will be free for all record types. We started piloting this approach with the back-year fee waiver for grant records introduced this January, and we’re now extending it to all members and all record types.

This is arguably the bigger of the two changes. For repositories, archives, national libraries, and other high-volume, low-budget collections—some of whom have told us plainly that Crossref was beyond their budgets—the cost of bringing decades of holdings into the scholarly record drops to zero. It also matters for existing members who haven’t had the chance to register historical records over the years, for archival digitisation projects, and for members absorbing another organisation’s unregistered backlist.

Why lower fees instead of continuing to invest?

Four reasons:

1. It’s not an either/or; we can reduce fees and invest.

We should be direct about scale and related plans: in consultation, members large and small told us frankly that a change from USD 1.00 to USD 0.95 wasn’t going to make a difference to them. We agree that five cents will not transform anyone’s budget. But it is a big change for Crossref, given our aim of deliberately reducing future surpluses in the years ahead and it signals our direction of travel.

2. It’s the responsible thing to do.

Crossref is in a healthy financial position. Revenue has grown steadily, through the growing volume of records registered with us and the long tail of thousands of new members rather than price rises. We met our target of holding twelve months’ operating expenses in reserve in 2023, and have run surpluses each year since. This puts us in the fortunate position of being able to lower our fees and still support our expenses. We act on our members’ behalf, so when revenue runs too far ahead of what’s needed to healthily run the organisation, the savings should find their way back to members. We’re also investing surplus funds into our systems and into community initiatives, through the framework our board agreed in November 2025; adjusting fees now is the other half of the same approach.

The combined changes are projected to reduce our Content Registration revenue by roughly USD 760K in 2027, around 5% of our total forecasted revenue. This is an intentional and manageable contraction, with growth in membership and record volumes expected to carry revenue forward from there, based on our 10-year revenue growth trend.

Taken as a package—alongside yesterday’s announcement of a major investment in redesigning our system—we think it’s a strong message from an open infrastructure: financial sustainability is a target to hit and maintain, and managing our finances responsibly and transparently is our obligation to the community.

3. It helps all members—and welcomes those we’ve been missing.

Nearly every member will pay less per record from 2027. And for the archives and repositories that register a great deal but operate on very little, free back-year registration removes what they’ve told us was the barrier to participating in Crossref at all.

4. It builds the Research Nexus.

More back-year records mean more raw material for our matching techniques, filling in more of the gaps and relationships in the scholarly record—citations, funding, affiliations, versions—which is a key goal. The record didn’t start two years ago, and neither should the metadata.

It was never just about the DOIs

It’s worth restating while we’re talking about per-record fees: registering a DOI has never really been the point. What members are paying for—and what the community gets—is the connective tissue of the scholarly record: 185 million records, more than two billion citation links, and metadata relationships spanning funding, affiliations, versions, and corrections—all matched and openly available to everyone through our APIs. It’s the metadata and the relationships, at that reach and scale, that turn a set of PIDs into a connected scholarly record. In fact, “PIDs are a means to an end” is the first of the six key positions in our new position paper on PIDs in research infrastructure policy, published this week.

The RCFS story so far

These decisions don’t stand alone; they’re the latest step in a program of work grounded in the fee principles our Board adopted in 2019 and formalised as RCFS following the July 2023 board meeting. The aims have stayed constant throughout: making fees more equitable, simplifying our complex fee schedule, and rebalancing revenue sources.

  • 2023: RCFS launched; Research Consulting helped us identify the changes with the highest positive impact, narrowed to five projects.
  • 2024: Research and consultation—surveys of over 1,000 members in the lowest fee tier, benchmarking against peer organisations, and interviews with non-member publishers about barriers to joining.
  • 2025: The first fee changes approved—a new USD 200 membership tier for the lowest-resourced members, removal of volume discounts, and simplified peer review fees, all effective January 2026.
  • January 2026: Grant registration fees halved and back-year grant fees waived as a two-year pilot to accelerate the Grant Linking System and the Research Nexus.
  • January 2027: Reduced Content Registration fees for nearly all record types, with free DOI registration for all back-year records.

What’s next

The RCFS project continues. Work remains on rebalancing revenue from the use of our metadata (including Metadata Plus fees), on normalising the annual membership fee structure for our funder members, on the fee calculator mentioned above, and on monitoring the effects of all of these changes, including whether free back-year registration does what we hope it will for the completeness of the scholarly record.

As ever, Board motions are publicly available and summarised on our blog.

We’ll be discussing these changes and other directions of travel for Crossref and the Research Nexus at our annual meeting and board election, Crossref2026, on October 22nd. Save the date and join us there.

Our thanks go to our internal team and the Membership & Fees Committee for the hours spent scrutinising data and asking questions on the community’s behalf. Questions and comments are welcome in the comments below or in the dedicated RCFS category on the community forum, or email us via feedback@crossref.org anytime.

Investing $4.9 million of our surplus in rebuilding the Crossref system

2026-08-12

The infrastructure that registers and distributes Crossref metadata has been running and growing for more than two decades. It now does considerably more than it was built to do, and it shows: two large, tightly coupled monoliths (Content System and REST API), where a change in one place risks breaking something in another.

In its July meeting, the Crossref board approved the use of Crossref surplus funds for a three-year project to accelerate the redesign of the Crossref system. Our plan is to use $1.6 million a year for three years (~USD $4.9M in total) from our surplus funds, which will help us set up a modern infrastructure consisting of separate services for the different functionalities that the Crossref system offers.

This infusion of time-limited resources will augment our existing team. We will continue to maintain the current system, add small improvements, fix bugs, and make schema changes. We’ve mapped out our time and capacity to ensure that we can keep the current system active and robust while building the new system at the same time. In the past, when we’ve attempted large redesign initiatives, we’ve done a code freeze on the existing system—we won’t be doing that this time. 25,000+ members and thousands of downstream services rely on the system daily, so we will continue to provide time and energy to support that while building for the future. As always, you can view our public roadmap to see progress with what we’re working on.

Background

Over the years, the board, leadership team, and staff have discussed the need to address long-term technical debt in the Crossref system and the need to move from a monolithic codebase to a more modern, manageable, resilient technology that recreates the Crossref system. We are aiming to do this through a set of connected services and functions that better meet our mission and enable the Research Nexus, our goal of a rich and reusable open network of relationships connecting research organisations, people, things, and actions.

Our colleagues Paul Davis and Sara Bowman described some of the current challenges and plans earlier this year in Hit refresh: redesigning our technical infrastructure. We migrated from a closed-source database to PostgreSQL in 2024, moved out of a physical data centre into the cloud in August 2025, and have steadily been deprecating older tools not fit for purpose, such as Event Data, Co-access and ‘old’ Metadata Manager while rebuilding replacements. That was some of the groundwork; now we’re rebuilding the centre.

The two largest components of the current architecture are the Content System (CS) and the REST API. Both function as monoliths. They use mono-repositories, and their internal components are tightly coupled. Over time, they have accumulated complexity, substantially expanding beyond their original purpose. Each of them is now responsible for many different functions, with different needs and characteristics. This monolithic and tightly coupled architecture makes it difficult to maintain, scale, or expand our system. Adding new functionality often requires workarounds, which further increases complexity and adds technical debt.

As a not-for-profit, any surplus we generate has three possible destinations: reserves, paying down debt, or reinvestment in the mission. We met our goal of holding twelve months of operating costs in reserve in 2023, and we carry no debt, so reinvestment is where the rest belongs. We aim to generate surpluses to ensure that we are operating within our means, but we don’t need to accumulate them.

Some of that reinvestment goes outside Crossref; this year we’ve supported other community initiatives and infrastructure that people rely on, totalling around $500,000. A large proportion of the rest should go where members will feel it.

Reinvestment also has to be the right shape. Under the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructure, time-limited funds are used only for time-limited activities: day-to-day operations are covered by day-to-day revenue, and one-off funds go to one-off projects. A three-year rebuild, staffed by fixed-term roles embedded in existing teams, meets that test.

What will we rebuild?

The approach we’ve taken is that we’ve identified the different functionalities of the current system, and divided these between the three programs. The three-year timeline refers to the duration of the additional investment that is now being made. We don’t expect all of the rebuild work to be complete within three years. And system maintenance and improvement is never done. The additional resources will accelerate our ability to address these projects and get them substantially complete or meaningfully underway within three years. We will start with the things that are easiest to separate out of the current system, and work our way through it. The deposit system will be the last part we’ll tackle— after the initial 3-year period. Exactly how we’ll break out or couple together its components will depend somewhat on how the rest of the work in all three programs proceeds.

Additionally, there will be work in the OSO program to pull out deprecated/unused code from the content system as other programs build new free-standing services.

Of course, an end goal is to rebuild all of this in open code repositories, and eventually turn our amber-coloured ‘open source’ to green in our biannual POSI audits.

What does this new investment and approach mean for our community?

Having seen the long list of things we’re planning to do, you may be wondering how this will impact you. Here are some things you should know:

You’ll be able to use our services throughout this project. Part of the reason why we are rebuilding different functions one by one is that we don’t want to interrupt the services we offer to our growing membership and users. The current services will continue to run until the new ones are in place, and we’ll have clear transition plans and documentation in cases where you need to update your workflows.

We won’t just rebuild like-for-like, but we’ll make improvements when we rebuild services. We want to continue to provide the functionality we currently offer to our members, but we also realise there are things that could be better. The clearest example of this is member self-service, which will be one of the main priorities for the CCT program next year. For example, as mentioned above, in 2025, about 15% of member support tickets were contact updates: changes members should be able to make themselves in seconds, but which our membership team handles by hand; that is the kind of thing that will improve life for both members and staff.

We are planning to deprecate some older/less-used services along the way, but we’ll give you plenty of notice. Crossref is probably running more services than you realise! We’re therefore evaluating all our services as part of the redesign, and keeping only the ones that are actively used and really add value for the community. If we decide to retire a service, we will contact all users so they have enough time to transition to an alternative service. Everything we’ve deprecated so far is also itemised on our website.

Community input will be a big part of the process. To ensure the services we offer meet your needs, we will need to keep talking with you. Our community managers and UX researchers will be reaching out to users of different services to understand use cases and ensure these continue to be met within the new system.

This blog is the first tagged ‘ system-redesign’ so follow this category for ongoing updates. If you have feedback or questions, please head to the dedicated community forum space for system redesign and ask us in the open.

Two halves of the same decision

Later this week we’ll also announce the latest round of fee changes resulting from our Resourcing Crossref for Future Sustainability (RCFS) work. What we charge, and what we do with what we collect, are two halves of the same decision; fees are to come down at the same time as we commit the largest single investment in our infrastructure we have ever made. Both follow from the same principle: we set fees to cover the cost of running the infrastructure, and when we hold more than we need, it goes back to the community and into the longevity of the scholarly record.

Our recent position paper on PIDs in research infrastructure policy argues that the value of an identifier lies in the metadata and services around it, and in whether those can be maintained over the long term. This approach to futureproofing open scholarly infrastructure is an example of that maintenance in action.

Share your feedback on the new Crossref Member Practices

2026-08-10

In December 2026, we plan to publish new Crossref Member Practices which will set out the standards we expect all Crossref members to meet. From August to October 2026 we are consulting with our members on a draft of these Member Practices. This blog provides the background to this, and explains how you can share your views.

In response to community concerns, in July 2025 our Board decided to update our Member Terms to require members to follow a broader set of Member Practices. As these are now drafted by a dedicated working group, we invite feedback on these draft Member Practices from all of our community by 9th of October 2026. Read on to learn more about the aims and development process of the Member Practices, and the different ways to offer your feedback.

Background on the development of Crossref Member Practices

Crossref membership is open to organisations that produce professional and scholarly materials and content. All members have agreed to our member terms, which outline both the benefits of membership and the responsibilities that come with it. We deliberately keep barriers to membership low, and we don’t assess the quality of our members’ outputs or the integrity of the research process as this isn’t part of our mission.

Instead, our role is to preserve the integrity of the scholarly record in a specific way. Our members can create and share Crossref DOIs – unique and persistent identifiers and metadata records for the objects they produce. These metadata records are openly shared through our free APIs, allowing the global scholarly community to discover, connect, and evaluate research.

Each DOI and metadata record confirms that an item has been published, provides a persistent link to the content, and identifies the organisation responsible for both the publication and the metadata. Members are encouraged to include rich information about their records—such as reference links, ORCID iDs for authors, ROR IDs for affiliations, funding information, licensing details, and other metadata—that provides evidence of their publishing practices and helps place their outputs within the scholarly record.

This information allows others to assess a publisher, its processes, and its content. A DOI itself is therefore not a mark of quality or endorsement - it simply confirms that an item has been published and provides metadata that can be considered alongside other trust signals when evaluating the content or the publisher. As we explained in our blog ISR part one: What is our role in preserving the integrity of the scholarly record?:

“Crossref is focused on enriching metadata to provide more and better trust signals while keeping barriers to membership and participation as low as possible to enable an inclusive scholarly record.”

Current member responsibilities

Our member terms have always required our members to keep their DOI records active, accurate, and up-to-date. Members are responsible for:

  • Registering accurate and timely DOIs and metadata records for the content they have legal rights to register.
  • Not registering records for content that they do not have legal rights to register.
  • Not registering duplicate DOI records for content that already has a DOI.
  • Not displaying DOI strings that have not been registered with Crossref.
  • Ensuring their DOIs continuously resolve to an active, publicly accessible landing page that displays the DOI following our display guidelines.
  • Updating metadata and posting a notice if content is withdrawn.
  • Promptly correcting any errors or inaccuracies in their metadata record communicated to them by Crossref.
  • Participating in Reference Linking by displaying their own and other members’ DOIs on any references provided in their own published content.

Why introduce additional Member Practices?

Over the past several years, conversations with the scholarly community have shown that people are interested not only in the metadata our members provide, but also in members’ broader practices. The community has continued to ask what Crossref can do when a member is not following responsible or ethical practices.

We also recognise that part of our role is helping members understand what it means to participate in the Crossref community. As we explained in ISR part two: How our membership approach helps to preserve the integrity of the scholarly record:

“Inclusivity is very important to us - after all, one of our organisational truths (the guiding principles for everything we do) is “come one, come all”, and this is mirrored in the POSI principles that commit us to broad stakeholder representation. We know that for new organisations, it may take them a while to be able to completely fulfil the membership obligations. We support them with information to help them understand what being a participant in the Crossref community entails. These organisations would have less of a chance of developing better practices if we were to limit membership in Crossref to ‘proven’ candidates.”

With this in mind, we decided to develop a set of Crossref Member Practices to clearly describe the expectations of membership. Our aim is to help our diverse members understand what responsible participation in the Crossref community looks like, regardless of organisation type, region of the world, or discipline. Additionally, where we see an ongoing pattern of non-compliance, particularly if a member does not make reasonable efforts to improve after concerns have been raised, this set of practices offers clear grounds for suspending or ultimately revoking their Crossref membership. We wanted to be clear that deceptive or fraudulent practices are not acceptable for Crossref members.

In July 2025, we updated our Member Terms to require members to follow new Member Practices. Since January 2026, we’ve been working in consultation with the Crossref Member Practices Working Group to develop the practices collaboratively. The working group brings together research integrity experts and scholarly sleuths at publishers, institutions, funders, and community organisations, including COPE and DOAJ. Together, they have shared different perspectives and challenges, helping to shape practical recommendations for the Member Practices. We would like to extend our thanks to this group for all of their suggestions and efforts over the past few months.

While these recommendations are mainly based on the most common issues that have been brought to our notice by the community and largely come from journal publishing, the Member Practices are meant to be adhered to by all Crossref members. These Member Practices will also be reviewed annually with the aim of updating them in line with trends that we notice in the community.

Throughout the group’s discussions, the group has also been clear about what will not change with the introduction of the Member Practices.

What hasn’t changed?

We are still unable to get involved in individual instances of non-compliant activity, we don’t intervene in legal or financial disputes between parties, and we don’t assess the quality of published content or research. But where we see compelling evidence of deceptive or fraudulent activities, where there are systematic problems indicating that a member is no longer acting responsibly, or particularly if a member does not make reasonable efforts to improve after being notified of concerns, we may suspend or ultimately revoke their Crossref membership.

  • Are the outlined Member Practices in line with your expectations of standard best publishing practices? Is there anything that surprised you?
  • Do the outlined Crossref Member Practices feel realistic and achievable? Are there any barriers to fulfilling them?
  • Are there any behaviours that have been discouraged in the Member Practices but are considered a standard practice in your community?

  • Do you need any more information or guidance that would help you in fulfilling any of the Member Practices?
